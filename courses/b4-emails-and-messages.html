Part 1 · the constraint that does the teaching

One phone screen, and what that forces 7 min live

Here is the rule, and it is not about brevity for its own sake: if your email does not fit on one phone screen, you have usually not decided yet. Length is where undecidedness goes to hide. Four consequences follow. The subject line carries the ask, so "Education proposal - decision needed Thursday" beats "Update on education" every time, because the first one survives a lock-screen notification and the second one asks the reader to open it to find out whether they care. The first two lines carry the message, because that is all many people read and all a preview pane shows. There is one ask, with a date and an owner. And anything that needs three asks is three emails or a meeting, never one email with three bullets, which is how asks quietly die.

LiveThe subject line is the ask4 min▶

Most subject lines name a topic. A topic tells the reader which folder the message belongs in, which is useful to nobody at 8:40 in the morning. Name the ask instead, and put the deadline in it:

  • "Update on education" - a filing label. The reader must open it to learn whether it concerns them, so it goes to the pile marked later.
  • "Education proposal - decision needed Thursday" - the same message, now readable from a notification. It has told the truth about itself before being opened.

Three habits worth stealing. Put the verb in: decision, review, approve, sign, no action needed. Put the date in, and make it a real date rather than "soon" or "EOW". And if the message genuinely needs nothing from the reader, say so in the subject line - "No action needed" is a gift, and people remember who gives it.

The test: read your subject line as though it arrived on a locked phone at the weekend. Does the reader now know whether to care, and by when? If not, the body cannot rescue it.

LiveWhy the first two lines are the whole message3 min▶

Preview panes show two lines. Phones show two or three. Readers who are deciding whether to deal with you now read exactly that far. So the two lines are not an introduction, they are the message, and everything after them is support for people who want it.

Which kills one habit permanently: the windup. "I hope this finds you well. Following on from our conversation last week, and further to the discussion at the steering group, I wanted to circle back on where we have got to with the education work." Four seconds spent, nothing said. The reader now knows only that you write emails.

The replacement is two sentences that would work as a text message: "The education proposal is slipping two weeks. I need a yes or no from you on Thursday." Everything else - why it slipped, what the evidence changed, what the risk is - lives below the cut line where it belongs. The reader who trusts you acts on line two. The reader who does not, reads on. Both are served.

Self-studyWhen the email should not be an email3 min read▶

The one-screen rule has a useful failure mode: sometimes the message will not fit, and that is information. Three cases where the answer is a different container rather than better prose:

  • Three or more real asks. Split it. One email per ask, each with its own subject line and owner, or a fifteen-minute meeting if the asks depend on each other. A single email with three bullets reliably gets one reply about the easiest bullet.
  • The reasoning is the point. If the reader needs to follow an argument to agree with you, that is a memo with a structure, not an email with paragraphs. b5 is next session and builds exactly that.
  • The message is a negotiation or a disagreement. Email freezes positions and creates an audience. b8 goes at this properly, including the cases where writing at all is the wrong move.

Notice that all three are decisions about format, made before any drafting. That is step 1 of the loop doing its job, and no tool can do it for you because it depends on things only you know about the reader.

Part 2 · the four parts of an ask

Asks that cannot be misread 7 min live

An ask has four parts and most asks are missing two of them. Name the person, because an ask addressed to a group is addressed to nobody. Name the action as a verb the reader can actually perform. Name the date, a real one. And say what happens if they do nothing, which is the part almost everyone omits and the part that makes silence expensive. Here is the uncomfortable observation that follows: most unanswered emails were never actually a question. They described a situation warmly and hoped the reader would infer the request. The reader did not infer it, and was not being difficult.

LiveName the person, the action, the date, the cost4 min▶

Take each part in turn, with the failure it prevents:

  • The person. "Could someone confirm the consent position?" has no owner, and diffusion does the rest. Write "Priya". If you do not know who owns the decision, that is the thing to find out before you write anything, and it is often the real work.
  • The action. A verb the reader can perform in one sitting: approve, reply yes or no, send me the figure, book the review. "Align on", "consider", "have a think about" and "take a view" are not actions, they are hopes.
  • The date. "Thursday 5pm" is a date. "This week", "ASAP" and "at your earliest convenience" are ways of asking without asking, and they transfer the awkwardness of the deadline onto the reader.
  • The cost of doing nothing. "If I do not hear by Thursday I will assume we hold the launch to October." This is the part that feels rude and is not. It tells the reader what their silence means, so silence becomes a choice rather than an accident. It also protects you, because you wrote down the default.

Four parts, usually two sentences. If yours needs a paragraph, the ask is probably still two asks.

LiveFYI, request, decision - say which one this is3 min▶

Three different messages, and half of workplace friction is sending one while believing you sent another:

  • FYI. No action wanted. Say that out loud - "no action needed, sharing for context" - and then genuinely do not follow up asking what they thought. An FYI with an implied expectation is the least honest message in the building.
  • Request. An action you want, on a timescale you can live with. Still needs an owner and a verb, and benefits enormously from a date even when the date is soft.
  • Decision. Something you cannot proceed without. Needs all four parts, and needs the default stated. This is the only one of the three where silence should cost the reader something.

Two practical moves. First, decide which of the three you are sending before you draft, because the register changes with it. Second, when your own unanswered email frustrates you, go back and read it as the reader did: nine times out of ten you sent an FYI and expected a decision.

LiveChat register, which nobody teaches you4 min▶

Most of us learned email at work by imitation and learned chat by accident, which is why so many messages arrive as a small formal email in a channel. Chat has its own register and it is not email with the paragraphs removed:

  • Shorter, genuinely. One or two lines. If it is running to five, it wants to be a document, and the fastest kindness is to say "writing this up properly, will send in twenty minutes".
  • No greeting ritual. "Hi Priya, hope you are well, quick one" costs the reader a line and buys nothing in a medium where your name is already attached. Start with the thing.
  • One thought per message. Not a wall with four questions in it. Send the question, let them answer, send the next. Threads are conversations, and a paragraph is a monologue.
  • Never use a thread to deliver something that deserves a document. Decisions, commitments, dates, anything a person will need to find again in six weeks. Chat is unsearchable in practice and unowned by design. Put it in a memo or an email, then link to it in the channel.
  • Say what you want in the first message. "Are you around?" is not a message, it is a request for a request. "Can you approve the scope change today - two minutes" is.

The failure this prevents is the most common one in modern work: an important decision that exists only as three messages in a channel, made by nobody in particular, findable by no one.

Part 3 · what the model is for

Good for drafting, useless for deciding 6 min live

Three things a model does well on this format, and two it cannot do at all. It is good at turning your bullet points into a first draft, which is the cheap step and genuinely a time saving. It is good at tightening a message you have already decided, under a hard word ceiling. And it is very good at rewriting the same message for a different reader - the same facts for your manager, for the client, for the engineer who will do the work. It cannot decide what the message is, because that depends on what you want to happen and who owes you what. And it is a net loss on anything three sentences long, where briefing it properly costs more than writing the thing.

LiveThe prompt pair: a professional email versus a real one4 min▶

The bad version is the one almost everyone types, and it is bad in a specific way: every slot it leaves empty gets filled with plausible vagueness.

Invites the fluent-and-empty draft

"Write a professional email about the education delay."

No reader, so it writes for everyone. No ask, so it closes with "please let me know your thoughts". No facts, so it produces "a number of factors" and "some early indications". No register, so you get the corporate default. No ceiling, so you get 220 words. It will be grammatical and warm and you will still have to write the email.

Every slot filled, so nothing gets invented

"Draft an email to Priya, our COO, who has ten seconds and will read it on a phone. The ask: approve the revised education scope by Thursday 5pm, and if she does not, we hold the launch to October. Use only these three facts - the proposal slips two weeks; the evidence says institutions hold the budget rather than students; procurement at these institutions runs 6 to 12 months, from a study of 31 of them. Register: direct, no greeting ritual, no closing thank-you paragraph, contractions fine. Ceiling: 110 words. Put the ask in the first two lines. Do not add any fact I have not given you, and if something is missing, list it at the end instead of filling the gap."

The last sentence is the load-bearing one. It converts the model's most dangerous instinct - filling a gap with something plausible - into a list of questions you can answer. That single instruction is worth more than any phrasing improvement you will ever make.

LiveThe ask line: vague versus unmissable3 min▶

Same message, and the difference is entirely in whether the four parts are present.

Cannot be acted on, and will not be

"It would be great to get some alignment on the education scope in the next little while if possible, so do let me know your thoughts when you have a moment."

Person, action, date, and the cost of silence

"Priya: I need you to approve the revised education scope by Thursday 5pm. If I do not hear by then I will assume we hold the launch to October and tell the team on Friday."

Count what changed. The hedges are gone - great, some, in the next little while, if possible, when you have a moment. A person appeared. A verb appeared. A real date appeared. And a default appeared, which is the part that turns a request into a decision. It is twelve words shorter and about ten times more likely to get an answer.

Self-studyThe three-sentence rule, and other times to skip the tool3 min read▶

Briefing a model well takes about ninety seconds. That is a bargain on a 400-word memo and absurd on a two-line reply. The rule: if you can say it in a breath, say it in a breath.

  • Short replies. "Yes, Thursday works. I will send the revised scope tonight." Do not brief anything. Just send it.
  • Anything where the relationship is the content. Thanks, apology, condolence, praise, hard feedback. b8 argues this properly: the risk is not a bad draft, it is that the reader can tell.
  • Anything you cannot check. If the email needs a number you do not have, go and get the number. A model will supply a shape where the number should be, and a plausible shape survives review in a way a blank never does.

The corresponding case for the tool is worth being fair about. Rewriting one message for three audiences, without changing the facts, is real work that a model does in seconds and does well. So is turning six bullets into prose when you know exactly what the bullets mean. Neither of those is deciding, and neither is cutting.

What is written and what is measured The drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage for each missing practice. Every number in the scorecard is computed from the text above it: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic - a be-verb followed by a participle - so read its count as a flag rather than a census.

Real world

The email that gets no reply is almost never too long. It is missing an owner. Watch what happens in a real thread: a message goes to four people describing a situation and closing with "let me know your thoughts". Each of the four reads it, sees three other names, and reasonably concludes that somebody better placed will answer. Nobody does. Three days later the sender follows up with an apology for chasing, which teaches everyone that the deadline was never real. The fix is not a firmer tone or a longer explanation. It is one name, one verb, one date, and one sentence saying what happens if the date passes. That is four parts, two sentences, and it fits above the cut line with room to spare.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Open your sent folder and rewrite five subject lines as asks with dates in them. Then check which of the five original emails actually got a reply, and see whether the pattern is what you expected.
  • Find one email of your own that never got answered. Classify what you actually sent - FYI, request or decision - then rewrite the ask with all four parts, including the cost of silence. Do not send it; the point is being able to see the gap.
  • Use the good prompt from Part 3 on a real message you owe someone, with a hard word ceiling and the "list what is missing instead of filling it" instruction. Keep the list it gives you back: those are the facts you had not gone and found.
  • Pick your three longest chat messages from this week. Rewrite each as one line, and decide honestly whether any of them should have been a document instead. Bring one to b5, where we build the document properly.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your email will not fit on one phone screen. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Tightening sentences on an undecided email produces a shorter undecided email. Length is usually a symptom: either you have not decided what you want to happen, or there are two or three asks wearing one subject line. Split it, or go back to step 1 of the loop and decide.

2 · Which part of an ask is most often missing, and what does its absence cost?

Most writers manage a name and something verb-shaped. Almost nobody writes the default: "if I do not hear by Thursday I will assume we hold the launch to October." That sentence is what makes the date real, turns silence into a choice, and protects you by putting the fallback in writing. It feels rude and is not.

3 · Which of these is the weakest use of a model on this format?

Briefing a model properly takes around ninety seconds, which is a bargain on a memo and absurd on a sentence. B and C are the two genuinely strong uses: same facts, different reader, and bullets into prose. Both start from a message you have already decided, which is the pattern to look for.
