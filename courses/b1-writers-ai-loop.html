The writer's AI loop

A model will give you 200 fluent words in four seconds. Fluent was never the hard part. The hard part is being specific, being brief, and sounding like a person who has actually decided something - and a generator is structurally bad at all three, because it has no stake in the outcome and nothing concrete to say. Tonight you get the loop this whole track runs on, and a lab that scores real text on seven measures you can check yourself. The opening draft reads perfectly well and scores 48 out of 100. By the end of the session you will know exactly why.

Part 1 · the spine of the track

Decide, brief, draft, cut, own 7 min live

Five steps, and the model is genuinely useful in exactly one of them. Decide: what do you actually want to happen, and who has to do it? Brief: write down the reader, the decision, the ask, the facts and the register. Draft: get words on the page, yours or the model's - this is the cheap step. Cut: remove everything that is not doing work, which is the step nobody delegates well. Own: check the facts, check that it sounds like you, and take responsibility for having sent it. Steps one, four and five are where good writing actually happens, and none of them are a prompting problem.

LiveThe five levers of this track4 min▶

The loop is the process. The five levers are the practices, and they are the same five the lab measures - so every session you can watch the number move:

  • Brief - the reader, the decision, the ask. Without it the draft opens with a windup and closes without a request. (b2)
  • Voice sample - three paragraphs of your own writing, handed over with "match this register". Without it you get the corporate default: utilise, leverage, facilitate, commencement. (b3)
  • Structure - the shape decided before the drafting. Without it you get one undifferentiated wall, and the rhythm dies. (b5)
  • Specifics - the real numbers, names and dates. Without them a model writes plausible vagueness, which is its single most dangerous output. (b2, b5)
  • Cut - deleting the closing paragraph that restates the opening, and every hedge you would not say out loud. The biggest single jump on the ladder. (b7)

There is a sixth toggle in the lab: "sound more professional". It is not a lever. Press it on a finished draft and it costs you 36 points - we will come back to why that is the most requested edit in the world.

Self-studyWhen to skip the model entirely3 min read▶

The loop has a branch at step 3 that people rarely take. Skip AI drafting when:

  • The piece is three sentences long. Briefing a model properly takes longer than writing it. Anything you can say in a breath, say in a breath.
  • The writing IS the thinking. If you do not yet know what you believe, drafting is how you find out, and outsourcing it means you arrive at the model's position instead of your own.
  • The relationship is the content. Condolence, apology, praise, hard feedback. b8 goes at this properly - the risk is not a bad draft, it is that the reader can tell, and what they learn is that you did not spend yourself on it.
  • You cannot check the facts it would need. A model will fill a gap with something plausible, and plausible is worse than blank because it survives review.

Skipping the tool is a competent choice, not a purist one. The people who get the most from AI writing are the ones with a clear rule for when not to use it.

Part 2 · the central idea

Fluent is not the same as useful 6 min live

We were all trained to spot bad writing that looks bad - typos, tangles, clumsy sentences. AI has made that category almost extinct and replaced it with something harder to catch: prose that is smooth, grammatical, warm in tone and carries no information. Nobody flags it in review, because there is nothing to point at. The scorecard in tonight's lab exists to give you something to point at.

LiveThe seven measures, and why these seven4 min▶

Each one catches a habit that fluent-but-empty writing has. None of them requires taste, which is the point - you can run them on your own draft without asking anyone's permission:

  • Hedges and filler - just, really, somewhat, arguably, I think, a number of. Each one moves the sentence a little further from a commitment.
  • Passive voice - "a decision was reached" tells the reader nobody is accountable. Counted here with a be-verb-plus-participle heuristic, so treat it as a flag rather than a census.
  • Rhythm - the spread of sentence lengths. Human writing varies; generated prose settles into a steady 18-to-22-word hum, and that hum is the most reliable tell there is.
  • Nominalisations - abstract nouns built from verbs: implementation, utilisation, finalisation. They hide who did what.
  • AI-tell phrases - delve, leverage, seamless, landscape, it's worth noting, do not hesitate. Not wrong, just fingerprints.
  • Reading grade - a real Flesch-Kincaid calculation with syllable counting. Work writing above grade 12 is usually not sophisticated, just unedited.
  • Specifics - the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. This is the one that separates a real update from a warm noise.
Self-studyWhat the score is not3 min read▶

Be careful with this tool, in three specific ways:

  • It is not a style judgement. Great writing breaks every one of these habits deliberately. A long flat sentence can be a device; a hedge can be honesty; the passive is correct when the actor genuinely does not matter. The score flags patterns, and you decide which ones you meant.
  • It cannot see whether you are right. A confident, specific, beautifully cut paragraph of nonsense scores extremely well. Accuracy is step 5 of the loop and no measurement replaces it.
  • It is a drafting tool, not a verdict. Run it on something you are working on, not on the piece you already love. Chasing a number through five revisions produces its own kind of dead prose.

Used properly it does one useful thing: it turns "this feels a bit waffly" into "eleven hedges, no numbers in six sentences, and every sentence the same length". You can act on the second one before breakfast.

Score a real draft, live ★ 12 min · everyone runs it

Below is a Cadence update as an unbriefed model would write it. Read it first, honestly: it is not embarrassing. It is grammatical, polite and entirely reasonable. It also scores 48 out of 100, runs to 223 words, and does not tell the reader what to do. Then start turning levers on and watch which of the seven rows moves.

All levers off: 48/100, 223 words. Read the seven rows. Note specifics at zero and eleven hedges - and note that passive voice and rhythm are already fine, which is why nobody catches this draft in review.

Turn on Brief: 52. Small, and instructive - the point moves to the front, but vagueness survives a brief if you did not supply facts.

Add Voice sample: 68. The biggest early jump, because the corporate register was carrying most of the passives and half the tells.

Add Structure (70), then Specifics (83). Watch the specifics row climb as numbers and names arrive.

Add Cut: 98. Half the words of where you started, twice the score. Then press Sound more professional and watch 36 points leave.

What is written and what is measured The drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage for each missing practice. Every number in the scorecard is computed from the text above it: hedge and tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. Change one lever and the text and the numbers change together.

The climb, then your own writing ★ 10 min · explore

The whole ladder, computed live: 48 → 52 → 68 → 70 → 83 → 98, with the word count falling from 223 to 102. That relationship is the whole course in one line - the score goes up as the words come down. Then the anti-lever: from 98, "sound more professional" drops you to 62 and adds 42 words, because every substitution it makes is a hedge, a passive or an abstract noun.

Read the rungs as sessions: brief (b2), voice (b3), structure (b5), specifics (b2 and b5), cut (b7).

Note where the money is: voice +16 and cut +15. Two habits, half the total climb, and neither is a prompting trick.

Real world

"Make it more professional" is the most expensive four words in written work. Watch what the anti-lever actually does: "I need a decision" becomes "it would be appreciated if we could obtain a decision", "runs 6 to 12 months" becomes "is estimated to be in the region of", and a two-sentence ask grows a "furthermore". Every one of those substitutions removes a person from the sentence. What people mean when they ask for professional is usually "make me less exposed" - and the reader pays for that in time and confusion. The professional version of a hard message is short, specific and signed.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Run the full ladder until you can predict which row moves before you click. Then take a finished draft to 98 and press the anti-lever, so you feel the loss in your own hands.
  • Paste three things you actually wrote this week into the score-your-own box. Record the three numbers. Do not fix anything yet - b7 is where the cutting is taught properly.
  • Take the worst of the three and delete 40% of the words without deleting any information. Score it again and write down what the difference cost you in time.
  • Write your own skip rule: three kinds of writing you will not hand to a model. Bring it to b8, where we test whether it survives a genuinely hard message.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the five-step loop, which step is a model genuinely good at?

Drafting is the cheap step and the model is excellent at it. Deciding, cutting and owning are where the writing is actually made, and none of them improve with a better prompt. In practice models are notably bad at cutting, because they were trained to be helpful and adding words feels helpful.

2 · Why does fluent-but-empty writing survive review when clumsy writing does not?

You can circle a tangled sentence. You cannot circle a missing number. That is exactly why the seven measures matter: they convert an absence into something nameable, which is the only form a reviewer can act on.

3 · From a 98, "sound more professional" drops the score to 62 and adds 42 words. Why?

Look at the actual swaps: "I need" becomes "it would be appreciated if we could obtain", "runs" becomes "is estimated to be in the region of". Each one adds hedging, passivity or abstraction. Formality is not the problem - evasion wearing formality's clothes is.
