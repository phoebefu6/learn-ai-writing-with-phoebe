Reading a document a model touched

a1 gave you the diagnosis: the failure you now approve is an absence rather than an error. This session gives you the pass that finds it - seven checks, run in a fixed order, in about a minute at manager altitude. The structural point is the one to take away: two of the seven send the draft back, because they mean the underlying work is not done and no amount of editing will rescue it. The other five the writer fixes in ten minutes. You will run the pass on a document you actually received, in the lab below, and leave with one sentence you can send instead of "it feels a bit long."

Part 1 · the review standard

The seven measures at a manager's altitude 12 min live

The writer track measures seven things and computes a score. You do not need the score. You need the same seven checks in a running order, because the order is what makes the pass fast: the first two decide whether you read the rest at all. Run them like this. One: can you find the ask? Two: are there numbers and names? Three: how many hedges? Four: who is doing the verbs? Five: does the rhythm vary? Six: abstract nouns and tell phrases? Seven: reading grade, as a sanity check rather than a verdict.

LiveWhy checks 1 and 2 are a different species5 min▶

This is the structural idea of the session, and it is worth being slow about, because getting it wrong is the most common way a manager becomes the bottleneck in their own team.

  • A missing ask is not a writing problem. It means nobody decided what the writing was for. You cannot edit a decision into a document. If you try, you will invent the ask yourself - and now you have written the thing, taken the risk of guessing wrong, and taught the writer that the decision step is optional.
  • Missing numbers and names are not a writing problem either. They mean the work underneath is not done. Nobody went and found out how many accounts are affected, or what procurement actually said, or whether the assumption was ever tested. No editing pass produces a fact. A model will produce something plausible in the gap, which is strictly worse than a blank, because plausible survives review.
  • Checks 3 to 7 are all editing problems. Hedges, passives, flat rhythm, abstract nouns, tell phrases, reading grade. Every one is fixable by the writer in about ten minutes with no new information, which is exactly why you should not spend your own time on them.

So the rule is a routing rule. 1 or 2 fails, the draft goes back. 3 to 7 fail, you name them in one line and the draft goes back with a deadline, because it is ten minutes of work. Either way it goes back, and either way you have not rewritten it.

LiveRunning the pass in sixty seconds4 min▶

You do not read the document to do this. You scan it, in this order, and you can do it on a phone:

  • Ask (10 seconds). Read the first three sentences and the last three. If the ask is in neither place, it is not in the middle either. Documents that bury their ask do not bury it in paragraph four - they do not have one.
  • Specifics (20 seconds). Pick any paragraph and count sentences carrying a number, a name, or a date. Under a third of the sentences and the risks in that document are reading as feelings.
  • Hedges (10 seconds). Scan for "somewhat", "arguably", "a number of", "we are continuing to explore". Each is a place where someone declined to commit.
  • Verbs, rhythm, nouns (20 seconds). One paragraph is enough. Are things happening to nobody? Are all the sentences the same length? Is anything being utilised, leveraged or finalised?

Grade you can skip most weeks. It is the sanity check for when something reads as heavy and you cannot say why, and it is the one measure people over-trust: a low grade is not a virtue and a high grade is not sophistication, it is usually just an unedited long sentence with three abstract nouns in it.

Self-studyThe four Cadence documents3 min read▶

Run the pass mentally on the week from a1. The education proposal has slipped, the evidence says the real buyer is procurement rather than the head of department, one pricing assumption was never tested, and a decision is needed Thursday. Four documents arrive.

  • The status update. Fluent, 240 words, three paragraphs. No ask. Check 1 fails: this is a description of a situation, not a request. Send back with one question: what do you want decided on Thursday?
  • The revised proposal. Has an ask, clearly stated. Two of nine sentences carry a number. Check 2 fails: the pricing case rests on the untested assumption and the document does not say it is untested. Send back - that is a research task, not an edit.
  • The buyer memo. Ask present, specifics good, eleven hedges and every sentence 19 words long. Checks 3 and 5 fail, and both are ten-minute fixes. Name them, give it back, ask for it before lunch.
  • The technical note. Grade 15, dense, and correct. Check 7 flags it, checks 1 and 2 pass. This one you read properly, because heaviness here is a symptom of a real thing being explained.

Notice how the pass sorted them: two are not ready, one is ten minutes from ready, and one deserves your actual attention. That sorting is the value, and it took a minute per document.

Part 2 · what you actually say

The review conversation 9 min live

Finding the problem is the easy half. The hard half is saying it in a way that produces a better document rather than a defensive writer and a second draft with the same absence in it. There is a form that works, and it is boringly consistent: two things, both checkable, and a prediction about what the fix will also solve. Compare these two messages about the same draft.

Vague and unactionable

"Thanks for this. I think it needs a bit more work - it feels long and I am not quite sure what the takeaway is. Maybe tighten it up?"

Two things, both checkable

"Two things. I cannot find the ask - what do you want decided, and by when? And only two of the nine sentences carry a number, so the risks read as feelings rather than facts. Fix those two and I suspect the length problem solves itself."

Everything that makes the second one work is structural rather than tonal. It names two things, so the writer knows when they are done. Both are checkable in front of you, which means neither can become a debate about taste. It asks a question the writer has to go and answer rather than issuing an instruction they can comply with cosmetically. And the last clause hands them a reason to believe the fix is worth it.

LiveMost length complaints are specificity complaints4 min▶

"It feels long" is the most common thing managers say about writing and one of the least useful, because it points at the symptom that is easiest to treat cosmetically. Here is the mechanism.

A piece that says nothing has to keep talking. It has no facts to anchor it, so it cannot land and stop. It restates its opening in its closing paragraph. It fills the middle with sentences that would be true of any document about any project. It adds transitions between points that are the same point. All of that padding exists because the document has nothing specific to be brief about.

Ask for shorter and you get the same emptiness at 60% scale, which reads slightly worse because now it is terse and vague. Ask what the reader is meant to decide, and which numbers support it, and the padding loses its function - there is no room for a restating paragraph once the document has a job.

This is also why the writer track's lab climbs from 48 to 98 while the word count falls from 223 to 102. Nobody in that sequence was asked to be shorter. They were asked to be specific and then to cut what was not doing work, and length took care of itself. Learn to distrust your own instinct here: when you feel "long", check specifics first.

Self-studyNaming the absence without naming the person3 min read▶

Absences feel personal in a way errors do not. "This sentence is tangled" is about a sentence. "There is no ask here" sounds like "you did not think." So the language matters more than usual, and the fix is grammatical: make the document the subject, not the writer.

  • "I cannot find the ask" rather than "you did not include an ask". The first is a report on your experience as a reader, which is unarguable and also literally your job.
  • "Two of nine sentences carry a number" rather than "this is vague". A count is a fact you can look at together. Vague is a verdict.
  • "What do you want decided, and by when?" rather than "please clarify the purpose". A question with two slots gets answered. An instruction to clarify gets an extra sentence in paragraph one.

One more thing worth doing out loud: when the answer to "what do you want decided" is "I do not know yet", that is a completely legitimate answer and you should treat it as one. The document was premature, not bad. Say so, and the next one arrives after the decision instead of instead of it.

Part 3 · the three traps

What not to do 7 min live · open all three

Three moves that feel like good management and reliably make things worse. The first is the one everyone reaches for in the first month, and it is the one I would most like you to give up tonight.

LiveDo not ask "did AI write this?"3 min▶

Four reasons, in ascending order of importance.

  • You cannot reliably tell. The tells in check 6 are fingerprints, not proof. Plenty of careful human writers say "furthermore" and plenty of model output does not. Your confidence in your own ear is not calibrated, and you will find out which way only when you accuse the wrong person.
  • Detectors do not work. This course makes no claim that any tool can identify AI writing, because none can do it reliably. Acting on a detector's output is acting on a coin flip with a percentage printed on it.
  • It puts people on the defensive instantly. The question is heard as an accusation of dishonesty, so you get a conversation about process and trust rather than about the document. Nothing about the draft improves.
  • It is the wrong question. This is the real one. A person can write fluent emptiness entirely unaided, and people did, for decades, before any of this existed. The tool did not create the failure - it lowered the cost of producing it. If your review standard only catches machine-written emptiness, it lets the human kind through, and there is plenty of that.

Ask for the brief instead. "Who is this for, what do you want them to decide, and what are the three facts it rests on?" That question is answerable, it is not an accusation, and it works identically whether the draft came from a model, an intern or a template from 2019. If the brief exists, the tool did not matter. If it does not, you have found the actual problem.

LiveDo not fix it yourself2 min▶

It is Thursday, the document is nearly right, and you can repair it in eleven minutes. You know how this ends: you repair it. It goes out. It was fine.

What you actually did was teach your team the real rule of your review process: send the manager a first draft and it comes back finished. Nobody decides that consciously and everybody learns it within about two months. The observable symptoms are drafts arriving earlier and rougher, your own evenings filling with other people's prose, and your best writers slowly getting less good at the last 20% because they never do it.

There are real exceptions and you should name them out loud so they stay exceptions: the deadline is genuinely tonight, the document is genuinely one paragraph, or the fix requires a fact only you have. Do it, then say plainly what you changed and why, so the learning still happens somewhere.

LiveDo not comment on style when the failure is substance2 min▶

The most demoralising review a writer can receive is eleven comments about word choice on a document that had no ask. It signals that you read the whole thing carefully and never noticed the hole - and it produces draft two with better adjectives and the same hole.

The discipline is the routing rule from Part 1. If checks 1 or 2 failed, say only that. No comma comments, no register suggestions, nothing about "delve". Those notes are not wrong, they are just noise on top of a finding that makes them irrelevant, and mixing them in lets the writer choose the easy ones.

Style notes have their moment: after the document has an ask and some facts, when they are the only thing left. Which, conveniently, is also the moment they take ten minutes and the writer can do them without you.

Build-along

Run the pass on something from your own inbox ★ 13 min · everyone runs it

This is the clarity lab from the writer track, in score-your-own mode. Do not paste an example - paste a document you received recently and were expected to approve. That is the whole point: the pass has to work on your real inbox rather than on a teaching sample. Nothing leaves your browser. Then write the two-things sentence for it, in the room, before you look at what anyone else got.

Find a real one. An update, a proposal, a memo, a review - something fluent that you approved or nearly approved in the last two weeks. Paste it in.

Before you read the rows, answer check 1 and check 2 yourself: can you find the ask, and how many sentences carry a number or a name? Write your two answers down first.

Now read the specifics row and the hedges row only. Ignore the total score - it is a writer's tool. Compare the specifics figure with your own guess; most people over-estimate by a lot.

Read your hedge list aloud to the person next to you. This is the moment the session usually lands, because hedges are invisible until they are a list.

Write the two-things sentence you would actually send. Two findings, both checkable, plus one clause predicting what the fix will also solve. One sentence each, out loud, round the room.

What is written and what is measured Be honest with your team about this tool, because they will ask. Every number in the scorecard is computed from the text you paste: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density (with a whitelist, so ordinary words like "evidence" and "institutions" are not punished), a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The example drafts in the writer track are written examples - one good version plus one specific kind of damage per missing practice - but nothing about the measurement is faked. One caveat to state plainly: the passive check is a heuristic, a be-verb plus a participle, so treat its count as a flag rather than a census. And the score cannot see whether the document is correct. A specific, beautifully cut paragraph of nonsense scores very well.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Run the sixty-second pass on every document you are asked to approve this week. Keep a tally of which check failed first. If checks 1 and 2 are failing more than half the time, your problem is upstream of writing and a5 is where you fix it.
  • Send exactly one two-things message. Two findings, both checkable, one clause predicting what the fix also solves. Note how long the reply takes to arrive compared with your usual review note.
  • Pick a draft you would normally have fixed yourself and send it back instead, with a deadline attached. Write down what you were afraid would happen, then write down what did.
  • Replace the "did AI write this" question for good: ask one writer for the brief behind a piece - reader, decision, three facts. Bring what they say to a3, because it tells you whether the house voice exists anywhere other than your head.

Real world

Almost nobody checks, and it is not carelessness. The Nielsen Norman Group's two-week diary study, published 24 September 2023, logged 18 participants and 425 conversations with generative tools. Only 22.43% of those conversations were followed by any verification at all. That is the strongest number available on this, and the reason it matters here is that your review is the last place the check can happen. Nothing in the reading experience of a fluent document prompts a reader to go and look something up - fluency actively suppresses the impulse, because the writing sounds like someone who already knew. Which is exactly why check 2 counts sentences instead of trusting your impression, and why it sends the draft back rather than inviting you to go and verify it yourself.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why do checks 1 and 2 send the draft back, while checks 3 to 7 do not?

You cannot edit a decision into a document, and no editing pass produces a fact. Hedges, passives, rhythm, abstract nouns, tells and grade are all fixable by the writer in ten minutes with no new information - which is exactly why they are not your job.

2 · What is the strongest objection to asking a writer "did AI write this?"

A and B are both true and both worth saying. C is the one that survives every counter-argument: even a perfect detector would not help you, because the failure you care about existed long before the tool and is produced by humans every day. Ask for the brief instead - reader, decision, three facts. It works identically whatever produced the draft.

3 · A document feels far too long. What do you check first?

Ask for shorter and you get the same emptiness at 60% scale. With no facts to anchor it, a document cannot land and stop, so it restates its opening in its closing paragraph. Fix the specifics and the padding loses its function - which is why the lab climbs 48 to 98 while the words fall from 223 to 102, with nobody ever being asked to be brief.
