Raising the bar without adding process

Last session of the leader track, so this one is about the year rather than the week. Two ideas carry it. First, measure the right thing: once drafting is free, volume metrics rise on their own and prove nothing, so you need a small set of quality measures you can actually count. Second, and less comfortably, the way to raise writing quality is usually to remove process rather than add it. The lab's ladder is the evidence sitting underneath both: the score climbs from 48 to 98 while the words fall from 223 to 102, so any process that rewards volume is working against you.

Part 1 · what to count

Measure the right thing 15 min live

The metrics that arrive first are the ones that are already instrumented: documents produced, response time, drafts per person. They will all improve. They will improve whether or not the writing got better, because drafting stopped being expensive, and a metric that goes up when nothing real changed is worse than no metric at all - it buys you a year of false confidence. The alternative is four measures that track what a reader actually experiences: does the reader know what to do, specificity, rework, and length discipline. One of them is judged by a human, and I will be straight about that rather than pretending otherwise.

LiveThe four measures, and how to score the human one6 min▶

Four, not fourteen. Each one maps to something a reader feels, and three of the four are countable by a script:

  • Does the reader know what to do. The share of documents where the ask is findable in the first two lines. This one needs a human, and here is how to make it repeatable: take ten documents a month at random, have two people read only the first two lines and write down what they think is being asked, then compare both answers with what the author says the ask was. Score the share where both readers matched. Same two readers each month, same sample size, and record the disagreements rather than averaging them away.
  • Specificity. The share of claim sentences carrying a number, date, count or name. Countable in the same ten-document sample, and the measure that most reliably changes behaviour, because complying usually means going and finding something out.
  • Rework. Review rounds per document, from whatever tool already records send-backs. It is the cheapest of the four and it moves when the standard starts working.
  • Length discipline. Median length per format, tracked monthly, and you want the line going down. Not one global average: an email and a decision memo have nothing to say to each other.

Report all four monthly on one line each, next to a sentence saying what you think caused the movement. If you cannot write that sentence, do not report the number yet.

Self-studyWhat the evidence licenses, and what it does not4 min read▶

If you are going to put numbers in front of an executive, be careful about which claims the research actually supports.

  • Quality can go up and sameness can go up together. Doshi and Hauser, in Science Advances in July 2024, ran 293 writers and 600 evaluators across 3,519 evaluations. Story novelty rose 5.4% with one AI idea and 8.1% with five, and the least creative writers gained most: novelty up 10.7% and usefulness up 11.5% in the five-idea condition. The same study found the stories became measurably more similar to one another within a condition. Both findings are real, and the second is the one your dashboard will never show you.
  • Do not overstate the similarity finding. The paper's percentages for similarity are expressed as a share of the total range of a similarity coefficient, not as "stories were 10.7% more similar". Say "measurably more similar to one another" and cite the paper. Getting this wrong in front of a technical audience costs you the whole argument.
  • Verification does not happen by default. Nielsen Norman Group's two-week diary study of 18 participants and 425 conversations found only 22.43% of conversations were followed by any verification. That is the number to quote when someone proposes a policy that relies on people checking.

What none of this licenses is a claim about how much time your team saves, or how many of your documents are AI-drafted. You do not have those numbers, nobody credible has published them for your context, and a made-up figure is the fastest way to lose an argument you were winning.

Part 2 · the counter-intuitive move

To raise quality, remove process 15 min live

The instinct when writing quality drops is to add a checkpoint. It never works, and the lab tells you why in one line: the score rises as the words fall, from 48 at 223 words to 98 at 102 words. Every layer of process you add rewards volume, because a writer facing an unknown committee pads defensively. So the moves that raise the bar are subtractions: take out a reviewer, give drafting time back, make cutting visible, and kill the ritual document that exists to prove effort rather than to inform a decision.

LiveCut a review layer rather than adding one4 min▶

Every additional reviewer does three things, and only the first is intended:

  • It dilutes accountability. With one reviewer, someone owns the send. With four, everyone assumes one of the others read it properly, and the failure mode is a document nobody actually read arriving with four approvals on it.
  • It lengthens the document. Writers pad to survive committee. If you do not know who will object, you pre-answer everyone, and the pre-answers are the padding. Ask any team that writes for a four-person approval chain and they will tell you exactly which two paragraphs exist for which person.
  • It slows the loop until the feedback stops teaching anything. Feedback that arrives nine days later does not change how anyone writes, because the writer has moved on and cannot remember the choice they made.

The move: pick the least valuable approval step you own and remove it this quarter. Name one accountable reviewer per document type. Then watch length rather than watching for disasters - if the documents get shorter, you removed the right step.

LiveProtect drafting time as a real calendar commitment3 min▶

The most common reason a document is bad is that it was written in fragments between meetings. Cutting in particular needs one uninterrupted stretch, because you cannot see what is redundant across nine pages in eleven-minute slices.

  • Make it a booking, not an intention. Two hours in the calendar, defended the way you would defend a client call. If it is negotiable, it is already gone.
  • Say out loud that drafting is work. Teams read the calendar to learn what their manager values. A manager who moves writing time first has told everyone that writing is the flexible part.
  • Match it to the standard's ceilings. A one-page update should not need two hours. If it does, the specifics are missing and the writer is padding around a gap, which is a content problem wearing a time problem's clothes.
LiveMake cutting a visible, praised act4 min▶

Cutting is invisible work with a bad incentive attached. Adding a section is legible: it is there, and it looks like effort. Deleting one leaves nothing to point at, and the writer risks being asked why the document is thin. So people stop cutting, and the lab's biggest single lever, worth 15 points, goes unused.

  • Praise the deletion specifically. "This is the same update as last month at 40% of the length and I understood it faster" is a sentence people repeat to each other for weeks.
  • Show a before and after in a team meeting, with the author's permission and their name on it. One live demonstration teaches more than any guidance document.
  • Never ask why a compliant document is short. That question, asked once, undoes a quarter of encouragement.

There is a second reason to make this visible. Anderson, Shah and Kreminski, at C&C 2024, found that participants using an LLM produced less semantically distinct ideas than with another creativity-support tool while producing more of them, and felt less ownership of what they produced. Ownership is the thing praise restores. Cutting is also the step where a writer takes a generated draft and makes it theirs, so praising it is not just about length.

LiveKill the status update that exists to prove effort4 min▶

Somewhere in your organisation is a recurring document that nobody reads and everybody writes. It survives because cancelling it looks like hiding. The test is one question: what decision does this change? If the honest answer is none, it is theatre, and now that drafting is free it is about to become longer, more frequent and more fluent theatre.

  • Ask the readers, not the writers. "When did you last act on this document?" Two rounds of that question usually settles it.
  • Kill it for one month rather than forever. Nothing happens, and the experiment is cheap enough that nobody has to lose an argument.
  • Replace it with the decision it was pretending to serve. Often that is a two-line message when something changes, and silence when nothing has.

This is the highest-leverage subtraction on the list, because it gives back the drafting time you were about to ask for in the previous card. You are not adding capacity, you are stopping a leak.

Real world

The cheapest demonstration you will ever run in front of a stakeholder. Take a finished draft that scores 98 in the clarity lab and press "sound more professional". It drops to 62, a loss of 36 points, and adds 42 words. Watch the substitutions: "I need a decision" becomes "it would be appreciated if we could obtain a decision", "runs 6 to 12 months" becomes "is estimated to be in the region of". Every swap removes a person from a sentence. It takes 90 seconds and it changes the conversation completely, because you are no longer arguing about taste with someone more senior than you. You have a scored baseline, a visible edit, and a number that moved in the wrong direction. That is the entire case for measuring anything: not to grade people, but to make a preference into a finding.

Part 3 · the year

The 12-month plan, one artifact per quarter ★ 12 min · plan yours

Four quarters, one shippable artifact each, and a re-measure at the end that rewrites the beginning. Nothing in this plan needs a new meeting, a new tool or a new approval step, which is deliberate: a quality programme that arrives as overhead gets judged as overhead. Each card below has what ships and what could go wrong, because the failure modes are more predictable than the successes.

LiveQ1 · Publish the voice doc and the one-page standard3 min▶

What ships: the voice doc from a3, built from real samples of your team's writing rather than adjectives, and the one-page standard from a5 with its seven blocks. Published together, called v1, with the date you will prune a third of it stated in the document itself.

What could go wrong: you consult too widely and the rules soften into aspirations, which is the failure a5 spent ten minutes on. Or you publish into a wiki and nothing changes, because the standard never reaches the moment of writing. Or the rollout is framed as a quality problem, which reads as an accusation and buys you six months of quiet resistance. Frame it as removing guesswork instead, which is also true: most people would rather be told the rule than guess at your taste.

LiveQ2 · Template the three formats, and measure a baseline3 min▶

What ships: templates for the three formats your team writes most often, with the relevant rule as a comment inside each slot, plus a first honest baseline of the four measures. Pick the metrics before you measure, and write down what you expect, so you find out later whether you understood your own team.

What could go wrong: the baseline looks bad and you decide not to share it, which quietly converts the programme into a private project. Share it, including the number you are embarrassed by. The other risk is templating the wrong three formats: template what is written weekly, not what feels most important. A quarterly board paper is not where the volume is.

LiveQ3 · Change the review habit and remove one approval step3 min▶

What ships: the two-things review form from a2 as the actual habit - name the ask problem, name the specifics problem, then send it back or fix it yourself - and one approval step removed, with a single accountable reviewer named per document type. Cutting gets praised in public at least twice this quarter, by name, with the author's permission.

What could go wrong: you add the review habit and keep all the approvals, in which case you have added process while telling everyone you were removing it, and people will notice. The other failure is removing a step and then quietly re-inserting yourself as the informal reviewer, which is the same step with less accountability and no record.

LiveQ4 · Re-measure, delete the unused third, change hiring3 min▶

What ships: the same four measures on the same method, next to Q2's baseline and next to what you predicted. Then the deletion you promised in Q1: every rule nobody cited, broke or remembered comes out, and you say publicly what was removed and why. Finally, the standard becomes part of hiring and onboarding - a short writing exercise scored against the seven blocks, and the standard plus the voice doc handed over together in week one.

What could go wrong: the numbers move sideways and you conclude the programme failed. Read the sample instead. Movement in the specificity measure is usually the first real signal, and the reader-knows-what-to-do measure lags it by a quarter because it depends on habits rather than effort. The other failure is skipping the deletion, which is how a one-page standard becomes a four-page one and then becomes nothing.

Homework

Try it yourself - this month ◐ 90 min total

  • Run the baseline once, properly: ten documents, two readers on the first two lines only, the specificity count, review rounds from the tool you already have, and median length per format. Write down what you expected before you look.
  • Publish the metric table with the gaming column intact, on the same day as the metrics. If you are not willing to publish the gaming column, you are not ready to publish the metrics.
  • Choose your subtraction for this quarter and only one: an approval step, a recurring document that changes no decision, or a defended two-hour drafting block. Write down how you will know within eight weeks whether it worked.
  • Run the 90-second anti-lever demonstration in front of one sceptical stakeholder. Take a draft to 98, press "sound more professional", read the substitutions aloud. Note what they say afterwards, because that sentence tells you what your organisation actually believes about writing.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why are documents produced and response time poor measures of writing quality?

They are the easiest metrics to collect and the least informative. A number that improves when nothing real changed is worse than no number, because it buys a year of false confidence. The four quality measures track what a reader actually experiences instead.

2 · What happens to document length when you add a fourth reviewer?

If you do not know who will object, you pre-answer everyone, and the pre-answers are the padding. Extra reviewers also dilute accountability - four approvals on a document nobody actually read is a common failure - and slow the loop until feedback stops teaching anyone anything.

3 · What makes the anti-lever demonstration useful with a stakeholder?

Without a baseline you are arguing about preference with someone more senior. With one you can point at the substitutions - "I need a decision" becoming "it would be appreciated if we could obtain a decision" - and say what each one removed: a person from the sentence. Formality is not the problem, evasion wearing formality's clothes is.
