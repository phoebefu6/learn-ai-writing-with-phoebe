Disclosure, trust and over-reliance

This is the session where I will be most careful about what is known. There are places where disclosing AI use is already mandatory, and the bodies that mandate it disagree about where you put it. There is a European law arriving on 2 August 2026 that does far less to your internal memos than the internet suggests. And there is the harder half, which no rule touches: what happens to a team's judgement when 22.43% of conversations get any verification at all. We will separate what is required, what is defensible, and what is simply yours to decide.

Part 0

Where this session sits, and one disclaimer

a3 gave your team a written voice. Tonight is the other half of trust: what you tell readers about how the writing was made, and what happens to your team's own capability when the drafting stops being theirs. The running case is still one week at Cadence, the AI meeting-notes company, where a research reference, a performance note and a customer-facing proposal all needed different answers to the same question in the same five days.

This is not legal advice. I am a practitioner, this is a training session, and where a rule genuinely does not apply to your situation I will say so plainly rather than let a vague sense of obligation do the work. If you need a position that will survive a regulator or a court, get counsel who has seen your contracts.

The distinction that runs through the whole session Mandatory disclosure exists in academic publishing, research and some commercial research codes. Defensible disclosure is what you choose for ordinary work writing, where no rule exists. Confusing the two produces either theatre or panic, and both cost you credibility.
Part 1 · where the rules already exist

Mandatory today, and inconsistent about where 9 min live

Start with the ground that is settled, because it is narrower than people assume and more useful than they expect. Academic and research publishing has written rules already. Read them as five bodies that agree on the principle and disagree on the mechanics - which is the single most teachable fact in this session, because it tells you that even serious institutions had to make a local choice, and so will you.

BodyWhat it requiresWhere the disclosure goes
ICMJE Describe AI use, and note that nondisclosure "may require corrective action and may be construed as misconduct" "in both the cover letter and the submitted work" - two places
COPE Declare the use, and accept that AI tools "cannot take responsibility for the submitted work" Materials and Methods
Springer Nature LLMs "do not currently satisfy our authorship criteria", so they cannot be listed as authors Documented in Methods
Science AI may not be an author, and cited sources "may not be authored or coauthored by AI tools" Reaches into the reference list, not only the method
Elsevier A declaration with prescribed wording A separate published declaration in the article
ICC/ESOMAR Code 2025, Article 9(b) For commercial research: disclose whether AI "played a significant role... and to what extent human oversight was involved" To the client, as part of the research disclosure

Read the right-hand column again. Cover letter and the work. Materials and Methods. Methods. The reference list. A separate published declaration. To the client. Everyone agrees AI cannot be an author. Nobody agrees where the sentence goes. That divergence is not sloppiness - it is what happens when several careful institutions each optimise for their own reader, and it is the honest precedent for you writing your own rule instead of waiting for a standard.

Say this part clearly to your team: these are academic and research-publishing rules, not workplace law. None of them governs your status update. They matter to you for two reasons. If anyone on your team writes for publication, submits to a journal, or does commissioned research under the ICC/ESOMAR code, these bind directly. And if they do not, the rules still give you a defensible shape to copy: declare the use, never claim authorship for the tool, and keep responsibility with a named human.

LiveThe one principle all six share, and why it is the load-bearing one3 min▶

Strip out the location arguments and one sentence survives across all six: a tool cannot be accountable, so a person must be. COPE says it most bluntly - AI tools "cannot take responsibility for the submitted work". Springer Nature reaches the same place through authorship criteria. ICMJE puts teeth on it by naming nondisclosure as potential misconduct.

This is why "the AI wrote it" is never a defence and never will be. Not because a rule forbids the sentence, but because there is no mechanism by which responsibility could transfer. A tool has nothing to lose, nothing to apologise with, and no standing to be believed next time. Accountability cannot move to something that cannot be held to it.

Carry that into every internal decision you make tonight. Whatever you choose about disclosure, the ownership line does not move: the person who sent it owns every claim in it.

Self-studyWho on your team is actually inside these rules3 min read▶

More people than a leader expects, and it is worth checking rather than assuming:

  • Anyone submitting to a journal or conference, including as a co-author on someone else's paper. ICMJE, COPE and the publisher's own policy all attach.
  • Anyone doing commissioned or client-facing research under the ICC/ESOMAR Code 2025. Article 9(b) asks two things: whether AI played a significant role, and the extent of human oversight. Note that the second half is a question about your process, not about the tool.
  • Anyone writing a chapter, a standards contribution or a peer-reviewed white paper where the venue has its own policy. Read the venue's page, not a summary of it.
  • Nobody writing internal memos, status updates, customer emails or proposals, unless a client contract says otherwise. Check the contract; that is where a real obligation would live for you.

The Science rule is the sharp edge people miss: cited sources "may not be authored or coauthored by AI tools". A hallucinated citation is not merely an error there, it is a policy breach - and a model will produce a plausible one on request without hesitating.

Part 2 · where there is no rule

A defensible internal position ✎ 14 min decide-along

For the writing that fills your team's week there is no rule, and the absence is doing damage in both directions. Some teams disclose nothing and are quietly nervous. Others staple a footer to every document, which reads as either a boast or a warning and teaches the reader nothing. The way out is a single question, applied per document, and four lines you can hold to.

LiveThe four lines, and why each one earns its place5 min▶
  • Disclose when the reader's judgement depends on how the text was made. A reference letter, a performance assessment, a piece presented as personal reflection, a condolence note. In each of these the reader is partly evaluating the effort and the source, not only the content, so withholding the method misleads them about the thing they are actually weighing.
  • Do not perform disclosure on routine drafting. Nobody expects a status update, a meeting summary or a formatting pass to be artisanal. A footer there is noise, and noise trains readers to skip the footer that will one day matter.
  • Never imply a human review that did not happen. This is the line that actually gets teams into trouble, and it is not about AI at all. "Reviewed by the team" when one person skimmed it is a false statement whoever drafted the words.
  • Own the content regardless. Disclosure is information for the reader, not a transfer of liability. If the number is wrong it is your wrong number, and saying which tool produced it changes nothing about that.

Four lines fits on the same page as your team writing standard in a5, which is where it should live. A separate AI policy document nobody opens is worse than nothing, because it creates the appearance of a rule without the practice of one.

LiveEU AI Act Article 50 - what it does, and what it does not touch4 min▶

The accurate version, because the inaccurate version is everywhere. Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026, and it was not delayed by the July 2026 Digital Omnibus. If you heard that transparency obligations got pushed back, that is the part to correct.

What it actually binds is narrower than the headline. It obliges providers of generative systems to mark synthetic output, and deployers to disclose deep fakes. Those are two specific duties on two specific roles.

An internal memo is not in scope. Your status update, your customer email, your board paper: Article 50 does not require you to label them. Do not imply a legal duty that does not exist, and be especially careful not to borrow the law's authority to win an internal argument you should win on the merits. If you tell your team the law requires a footer on every document, you will be wrong, and the day someone checks is the day your other guidance loses its weight too.

Two practical consequences that are real. If you build or resell a generative feature, the provider obligations may reach you and that is a counsel conversation, not a training-session one. And if anyone in your organisation produces synthetic audio, video or images of real people, the deep-fake disclosure duty is directly relevant to them.

Hedging footer - protects nobody

"Disclaimer: portions of this document may have been produced with the assistance of artificial intelligence tools. While reasonable efforts have been made to ensure accuracy, no warranty is given as to the completeness or correctness of the content herein, and readers should independently verify any information before relying upon it."

One honest sentence a reader can act on

"I drafted this with an AI assistant and then checked every number against the billing export and both dates against the signed SOW. The judgement about which option to recommend is mine."

Real world

Three documents, one Cadence week, three different answers. The customer proposal got no disclosure: it is a company document, the numbers were verified, and nobody was evaluating whose fingers typed it. The reference letter for a departing engineer got one sentence at the top, because the entire value of a reference is that a specific person spent their own judgement on it - and a generated reference is worth nothing to the reader even if every sentence is true. The research citation in the education proposal got the strictest treatment of all: the source was pulled up and read, because a plausible reference is the one failure that survives review and then detonates in front of a customer. Same week, same team, same tool, three correct answers. That is what a decision rule buys you over a blanket policy.

Part 3 · the harder half

Over-reliance, and where the next good writer comes from 11 min live

Disclosure is the easy half. It is a rule you can write in an afternoon. The harder half has no rule available and shows up on a two-year lag, which is exactly the timescale leaders are worst at. Three pieces of evidence, quoted as their sources frame them.

People do not check. The Nielsen Norman Group diary study of 24 September 2023 - two weeks, 18 participants, 425 conversations - found that only 22.43% of conversations were followed by any verification. Small sample, and it is the strongest available number of its kind. Roughly four in five outputs went unchecked by people who knew they were being observed.

The ideas converge and the ownership drains. Anderson, Shah & Kreminski, "Homogenization Effects of Large Language Models on Human Creative Ideation," C&C 2024, DOI 10.1145/3635636.3656204, ran 36 participants and found three things together: different users produced less semantically distinct ideas with ChatGPT than with another creativity-support tool, they produced more of them, and they felt less ownership of them. For a writing course the ownership finding is the one that matters. More output, less conviction, is a precise description of the documents that are hardest to review, because nobody in the room will defend a sentence.

Individuals gain and the pool narrows. Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances 10(28), eadn5290, 2024: story novelty rose +5.4% with one AI idea and +8.1% with five, and the least creative writers gained most. Stories also became measurably more similar to one another within a condition. Note carefully what I am not saying: the paper's similarity figures are a share of a coefficient's total range, so "10.7% more similar" is a misreading, and I will not use it. The honest form is "measurably more similar", with the citation attached.

Put the three together and you get the leader question, which I do not have a comfortable answer to. If nobody on your team drafts from scratch any more, where does the next good writer come from? The reps that made you competent were the bad first drafts you had to fix yourself. If those reps are gone, the deliberate practice has to be put back on purpose, because it will not return by accident - and the cost of not doing it lands in about three years, on whoever is running your team then.

Ask the one question on your next three real documents before you send them. Write down the answer. Three data points is enough to see whether your instinct and your rule agree.

Count verification on your own last day of tool use. Against 22.43%, are you better or worse, and would you say the number out loud in a team meeting?

Find the ownership signal in your last review cycle: a document where nobody in the room would defend a specific sentence. That is the Anderson finding arriving in your building.

Name one deliberate-practice rep you will protect - a weekly piece somebody drafts unaided - and say who it belongs to. Unowned practice does not happen.

Self-studyPutting the reps back without banning the tool4 min read▶

Banning drafting is not available to you and would not work if it were. Four interventions that do not require a ban:

  • One unaided piece a week, named. Small and real: a customer reply, a section of an update, the hard paragraph. The point is the struggle, so it must be a piece that matters enough to be difficult.
  • Draft-then-compare. Have the writer produce their own version first, then the tool's, then diff them. This preserves the rep and turns the tool into feedback rather than a substitute, which is also the only reliable way to notice voice flattening.
  • Make verification visible, not virtuous. "Which numbers did you check, and against what?" asked routinely in review. The 22.43% figure is not a character problem, it is a friction problem, and a standing question is the cheapest friction you can add.
  • Review for conviction. Ask the writer to defend one sentence they would fight for. If there is not one, the document is the more-output-less-ownership pattern and no amount of line editing will fix it.

Note the honest limit: none of these four is evidence-backed as a remedy. The problem is measured, the fixes are argued from mechanism, and I am telling you which is which.

Self-studyReading these studies without overclaiming3 min read▶
  • The NN/g study is 18 people and 425 conversations over two weeks. Use it as the strongest available "people do not check" number, not as a population statistic. It is directional and it is honest about being a diary study.
  • Doshi & Hauser is genuinely good news for individuals. Novelty up 5.4% with one AI idea, 8.1% with five, and the least creative writers gained the most. Do not present the paper as purely a warning; it is a trade-off finding, and the collective cost is the half people skip.
  • Do not say "10.7% more similar". That figure is a share of a similarity coefficient's total range, not a percentage increase in similarity. "Measurably more similar to one another" plus the citation is the accurate form, and someone in your audience will know the difference.
  • Anderson is 36 participants comparing against another creativity-support tool, not against unaided work. The ownership finding is the transferable one, and it is subjective by design because ownership is a feeling.

Precision here is not pedantry. The moment a leader overstates a study, the whole argument becomes contestable on a technicality, and the technicality is the only thing anyone remembers.

  • Write your four lines. When you disclose, when you do not, the never-imply-a-review rule, and the ownership rule. One paragraph each, and put it on the same page as the writing standard you build in a5 rather than in its own policy document.
  • Check who is actually inside a mandatory rule. Anyone publishing, anyone doing commissioned research under ICC/ESOMAR, and anything a client contract specifies. Read the venue or contract page itself, not a summary.
  • Correct one piece of Article 50 folklore in your organisation. It applies from 2 August 2026, it was not delayed by the July 2026 Digital Omnibus, it binds providers to mark synthetic output and deployers to disclose deep fakes, and it does not put a label on your internal memo.
  • Pick the rep you will protect. One piece of writing a week that someone on your team drafts unaided, with a name against it. Bring it to a6, where we look at what to stop doing to make room for it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · ICMJE wants it in the cover letter and the work, COPE in Materials and Methods, Elsevier in a separate published declaration. What is the teachable point?

The principle is settled everywhere: a tool cannot take responsibility, so it cannot be an author. The location is a local choice, and five careful institutions each made a different one. That is the precedent for writing your own rule rather than waiting for a standard, and remember these are academic and research-publishing rules rather than workplace law.

2 · EU AI Act Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026. What does it actually require?

Two specific duties on two specific roles, and it was not delayed by the Digital Omnibus. Your status update, customer email and board paper are outside it. Do not borrow the law's authority for an internal argument: the day someone checks, your other guidance loses weight too.

3 · Which of these is stated accurately, given the caveats in the research?
