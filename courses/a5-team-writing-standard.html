The team writing standard

This is the session that produces an artifact. By the end you should have a one-page standard your team can actually follow, written in wording you can paste. Most writing standards fail, and they fail in four predictable ways, so we will spend the first ten minutes on the failure modes and the rest of the session building the version that survives contact with a Tuesday afternoon. The test for every line is simple: it is either a rule someone can follow, or an example they can copy. If it is neither, it is a speech.

Part 1 · the failure modes

Why writing standards fail, and the shape that does not 10 min live

Nearly every team I have worked with has a style guide somewhere. Almost none of them changes what gets sent. The reasons are boringly consistent. It is too long to read, so it is read once at onboarding and never again. It is written as aspirations rather than rules: we write with clarity, warmth and precision, which nobody can act on. It has no examples, so two readers take opposite meanings from the same line in good faith. And it is unenforceable, because nothing in it is checkable, so a reviewer who wants to use it has only their own taste to fall back on. The fix is one test applied to every line: this is either a rule someone can follow on a Tuesday, or an example they can copy. Delete anything that is neither. One page is not a stylistic preference either, it is the enforcement mechanism: if a block will not fit, that is information about the block rather than about the page.

LiveThe four failure modes, named5 min▶

Worth saying these out loud with your team in the room, because most people have privately noticed all four and assumed it was just their company:

  • Too long to read. Length is the quiet killer. Anything past a page competes with the work, and the work wins. One page also forces you to choose, which is the useful part.
  • Aspirations instead of rules. "We write with clarity and warmth" cannot be complied with or breached. Nobody has ever changed a sentence because of it.
  • No examples. A rule without an example is an invitation to interpret. Two capable people will read "be concise" as 600 words and as 90, and both will feel compliant.
  • Unenforceable. If nothing is checkable, review falls back on taste, and taste review is where seniority beats reasoning. Checkable rules are what let a junior push back on a director politely.

The seven blocks in Part 2 are simply the smallest set I have found that closes all four holes at once, and every one of them is checkable by someone who has never met you.

LiveAspiration versus three checkable rules4 min▶

Here is the same intent written both ways. Read the first one and try to name a single sentence you would change in a draft because of it:

The style guide nobody can use

"We write with clarity, warmth and precision. Our communications should be reader-focused, respectful of people's time, and reflect the professionalism our clients expect. Wherever possible, avoid unnecessary complexity and aim for a tone that is both approachable and authoritative."

Three rules, each with an example

1. The ask is in the first two lines. Yes: "I need a go or no-go on the education pilot by Thursday 5pm." No: "I wanted to share an update on where things stand with the pilot."

2. Every claim that could carry a number carries one. Yes: "Setup runs 6 to 12 months across three institutions." No: "Setup timelines can be somewhat variable."

3. If you would not say a hedge out loud, cut it. Yes: "We do not know whether the smaller sites can staff this." No: "There may arguably be some resourcing considerations."

The second version is longer on the page and shorter in the head. That is the trade you want: specificity costs words in the standard and saves them in every document the standard touches.

Self-studyWrite it with three people, not thirty3 min read▶

Consultation feels safer and produces worse standards, because every additional reviewer adds a line and softens two, and softened rules are unenforceable by construction. Draft it yourself in one sitting: forty minutes, one page, from the wording in Part 2. If it takes longer than an evening you are writing a manual instead.

Then show it to three people who write a lot and ask one question only: which of these rules would you have broken last week, and why? That surfaces the unworkable ones fast. Do not ask for approval - announce the version. Call it v1 and say plainly that a third of it will be deleted in two months, which removes the incentive to argue every line now and converts a political negotiation into an experiment.

Part 2 · build the artifact

The seven blocks, with wording you can paste ★ 22 min · everyone drafts

Each block below has the actual wording, written to be pasted into your own document and edited rather than admired. Keep the order: the ask block first because it is the one that changes the most documents, and the delegation block last because it is the one people quote back to you when they need permission to write something themselves. Every block is short enough that seven of them fit on a page with room to breathe.

LiveBlock 1 · The ask goes in the first two lines3 min▶

The single highest-yield rule you can ship, because it is checkable in four seconds by anyone, including the writer.

Paste this

1. The ask goes in the first two lines, always. Say what you want the reader to do, and by when. If you want nothing, say that too: "no action needed, for your awareness." If you cannot write the ask in one sentence, the document is not ready to be written. Context, history and reasoning go after the ask, not before it.

Yes: "I need a go or no-go on the education pilot by Thursday 5pm. Three things changed this week."
No: "As you know, we have been exploring the education opportunity for some time now."

The windup exists because writers use the opening to warm themselves up. That is a drafting habit, and drafting habits belong in the draft. Cutting the first paragraph is right roughly nine times in ten.

LiveBlock 2 · The specifics rule4 min▶

This is the block that changes behaviour rather than prose, because complying with it usually means going and finding something out.

Paste this

2. Every claim that could carry a number carries one. Volumes, dates, costs, durations, counts, names of the people involved. If the number does not exist yet, the writing is not ready: go and get it, or write the sentence that says plainly what we do not know and what would settle it.

Yes: "Two of the three pilot sites can staff this in Q4. The third needs one more hire, roughly 8 weeks."
No: "Resourcing may present some challenges at certain sites."

The second clause is the important one, and it is why this rule is fair rather than punishing. You are not asking people to invent precision. You are asking them to stop dressing a gap as a sentence. A plain "we do not know X, and a two-day check with the delivery lead would settle it" is fully compliant and often the most valuable line in a document.

LiveBlock 3 · The banned list4 min▶

This is where the AI tells live. Not because these words are wrong, but because they are fingerprints, and a page full of fingerprints reads as nobody's writing.

Paste this

3. Banned words and phrases. Do not send anything containing: delve, leverage (as a verb), seamless, robust, landscape (figurative), underscores, it's worth noting, do not hesitate. Add our house entries: synergy, going forward, reach out, circle back, best-in-class, journey (unless it is a customer literally moving between steps).

If you need one of these words to say something you cannot say otherwise, use it and expect to be asked why. The list is not a filter, it is a prompt to write the sentence yourself.

Two things to say when you present this. First, the list must include house additions, because your team has its own tics that no generic list contains, and people trust a list far more when they recognise their own habits in it. Second, do not promise that changing models will fix the sameness. Wenger and Kenett tested 102 humans against 22 LLMs on three divergent-thinking tasks and found markedly lower semantic distance for the models, with effect sizes of 0.87 to 1.8 across model families. The tells are not one vendor's quirk, so a banned list is a house artifact rather than a vendor workaround.

LiveBlock 4 · The hedge rule3 min▶

Hedges are the most common form of the fluent-and-empty failure, and the read-aloud test catches them faster than any word list can.

Paste this

4. If you would not say it out loud, cut it. Read the draft aloud before sending. Anywhere you would not have said "somewhat", "arguably", "I think", "it may be the case that" in the room, delete it. Real uncertainty gets its own plain sentence instead: name what we do not know, and what would settle it.

Yes: "We have not tested the integration at more than 200 concurrent users. A one-day load test would tell us."
No: "The integration should arguably scale reasonably well in most likely scenarios."

The rule is generous in one direction, which is what makes it land. You are not banning uncertainty. You are moving it from a fog spread across nine sentences into one sentence a reader can act on. Writers usually find this liberating within a week, because hedging is exhausting and it never actually protected them.

LiveBlock 5 · Length ceilings per format3 min▶

A ceiling is the only length instruction that works, because "be concise" is a feeling and "one screen" is a fact.

Paste this

5. Length ceilings. Email: one screen, no scrolling on a laptop. Chat message: four lines. Weekly update: one page. Decision memo: two pages, with the recommendation on page one. Proposal: whatever the client asked for, and not a word more.

If it will not fit, do not shrink the font. Do one of three things: cut the background section, move the detail to an appendix or a linked document, or split it into two documents with different asks. Never send the over-length version with an apology at the top.

The "what to do when it will not fit" clause is what stops this rule being ignored. Without it, the ceiling reads as a wish and the first genuinely complicated document breaks it permanently. With it, breaking the ceiling becomes a decision with three named options rather than a quiet failure.

LiveBlock 6 · The templated formats3 min▶

Nobody should start from blank on a document your team writes every week. A template is a rule that has already been followed.

Paste this

6. Use the templates. Do not start from blank. We have solved three formats: the weekly update, the decision memo, and the client status note. Each has slots, and a slot with nothing in it is a question to go and answer rather than a line to delete.

Weekly update: the ask or "no action needed" · what changed, with numbers · what is at risk, with a name against it · what I need from you.
Decision memo: the recommendation · the decision needed and by when · the two or three options with what each costs · what we do not know.

Templates are the cheapest enforcement you will ever buy, because they move the rule from something people must remember to something the document already does. They are also the block that makes the standard popular rather than merely tolerated: the first person who fills in a memo template in eleven minutes instead of an hour becomes your best advocate.

LiveBlock 7 · What nobody delegates3 min▶

The writer track builds this list from the inside. In the standard it does a different job: it is the line people point at when they need permission to spend an hour writing four sentences themselves.

Paste this

7. What nobody delegates to a model. Four kinds of writing you draft yourself, every time:

Anything three sentences long, because briefing a tool takes longer than writing it.
Writing that is the thinking, where drafting is how you work out what you believe.
Anything where the relationship is the content: apology, condolence, praise, hard feedback, saying no.
Anything whose facts you cannot personally check, because a plausible gap survives review and a blank one does not.

The fourth item deserves the most airtime with a leadership audience, because it is the one people assume they already comply with. In the Nielsen Norman Group diary study of 425 conversations across 18 participants, only 22.43% of conversations were followed by any verification at all. Whatever we tell ourselves about checking, the observed rate is roughly one in five. a4 goes at that properly; here it is the reason block 7 has to be written down rather than assumed.

Order matters more than wording Change every sentence above to sound like your team. Keep the sequence. The ask rule first is what gets the standard used in week one, and the delegation block last is what gives people cover in month three. A standard whose first line is about banned words reads as a policing document, and it will be received exactly that way.
Part 3 · enforcement

Making it real, without becoming the writing police 7 min live

A standard is an artifact, and artifacts sitting in wikis do nothing. Four moves make the difference between a document people quote and a document people forget by Wednesday: put it where the writing happens, hand it to new joiners alongside the voice doc from a3, review against it rather than against your taste, and plan from day one to delete a third of it. The enforcement ladder below is honest about the limits: each layer closes a gap and leaves a smaller one, and no single layer is sufficient.

LivePut it where the writing happens2 min▶

The wiki is where documents go to be findable, not to be read. Move the standard to the moment of writing instead:

  • Inside the templates themselves. The relevant rule as a one-line comment at the top of each slot. This is the highest-yield placement by a distance, because it arrives at the exact second it is needed.
  • Pinned in the channel where drafts get shared, so the send-back reference is one click from the send-back.
  • In the document you already open weekly. If your team runs a weekly update doc, the four length ceilings live at the top of it.

One page in three places beats four pages in one place. Duplication is fine here as long as there is a single source you edit and the copies are links.

LiveNew joiners get the standard and the voice doc together2 min▶

The standard says what is required. The voice doc from a3 shows what it sounds like when it is done well, using real samples of your team's writing rather than adjectives. Handed over separately, the standard reads as compliance and the voice doc reads as optional. Handed over together in week one, they read as how we write here.

Give them a task, not a reading assignment: take a real document from the last month, score it against the seven blocks, and tell me which two blocks it breaks. Twenty minutes, and it teaches the standard from the inside. It also gives you a steady supply of honest feedback about which rules are unworkable, from the only people in the building with no stake in defending the current wording.

LiveReview against the standard, not against your taste2 min▶

This is the discipline that makes the whole thing worth doing, and it costs you something real: you give up the right to send a draft back because you would have written it differently.

  • Name the block, not the vibe. "Block 1: I cannot find the ask" is actionable and unarguable. "This does not quite land for me" is neither.
  • Two things, then send it. a2's send-back form: the ask and the specifics. Everything else you either fix yourself or let go.
  • If your objection is not in the standard, either add it or drop it. That is the honest test, and it will shrink your objections considerably.

The payoff is that people stop writing for you and start writing for the reader. Taste review teaches a team to model your preferences, which is a skill with no value to anyone outside the room.

Self-studyPlan to delete a third of it3 min read▶

Announce this when you publish v1, and put a date on it. After two months, go through the standard and cut every rule nobody has cited, nobody has broken, or nobody remembers. Expect that to be roughly a third.

The reason is not tidiness. Unused rules erode the authority of the whole document: once a team learns that some lines are decorative, every line becomes negotiable, including the three that were carrying all the value. A short standard where every rule has been used at least once is enforceable. A long one where half is dead is a document people have quietly agreed to ignore, which is worse than having none, because it lets you believe the problem is handled.

Keep the deletions visible. "We removed four rules this quarter because nobody needed them" builds more trust in the remaining rules than any amount of rollout communication.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 60-75 min total

  • Draft your one page in a single sitting, using the seven paste blocks as the starting wording. Change every sentence to sound like your team. Do not add an eighth block yet.
  • Run the rule-or-example test on every line you wrote yourself. Delete anything that is neither. Count what you deleted, because that number tells you how much aspiration you were carrying.
  • Add your house entries to the banned list by reading five real documents your team sent last month and writing down the words that appear in all five. Those are your tells, and they will not be on any generic list.
  • Pick your first enforcement layer and only that one. Put the relevant rule inside one template, pick the date you will re-read the standard in two months, and bring both to a6 where we work out what to measure and what to stop doing.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is the test for every line in the standard?

Aspirations cannot be complied with or breached, so they change nothing. A rule someone can follow and an example they can copy are the only two kinds of line that survive contact with a real afternoon. Broad agreement, meanwhile, tends to soften rules until they are unenforceable.

2 · The specifics rule says every claim that could carry a number carries one. What happens when the number does not exist yet?

That second clause is what makes the rule fair rather than punishing. You are not asking anyone to invent precision, you are asking them to stop dressing a gap as a sentence. "We do not know whether the smaller sites can staff this, and a two-day check would settle it" is fully compliant and often the most useful line in the document.

3 · Why plan to delete a third of the standard after two months?

Once a team learns that some lines are decorative, every line becomes negotiable. Announcing the prune when you publish v1 also converts a political argument into an experiment, which is why fewer people fight the wording on day one.
