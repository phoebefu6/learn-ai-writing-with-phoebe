The house voice, written down

Every team believes it has a voice. Almost none of them can produce the document. That was survivable when voice passed from person to person by osmosis over about eighteen months. It stopped being survivable the moment your team started briefing a tool that has never met you, cannot absorb anything, and defaults to the average of everything it has read. Tonight you write two pages that fix it. On the clarity lab's ladder the voice-sample lever is worth +16 points, tied with cutting as the largest single jump - so this is not a branding exercise, it is the highest-leverage practical artifact a leader can produce this quarter.

Part 1 · the problem, stated plainly

A voice nobody wrote down cannot be matched 7 min live

Here is the whole argument in one sentence: a voice that exists only in people's heads cannot be matched by a new hire or by a model, because there is nothing to match against. You already knew the first half. Onboarding has always been slow for exactly this reason. What changed is that you are now handing the same impossible task to a tool ten times a day, and it does not have eighteen months to soak in the culture. It answers immediately, in the register of the internet.

And the usual substitute makes it worse. "Professional, friendly, human" is three adjectives that cannot be wrong. Nobody can hold a draft up against them and say no. Ask five people on your team what "professional" means and you will get five registers: one means no contractions, one means no exclamation marks, one means hedge everything, one means shorter, one means Latin. An instruction that cannot be violated is not an instruction, it is a mood. The fix is boringly concrete: samples plus rules plus a banned list. Samples can be matched. Rules can be broken. A banned list can be checked by anyone in a text search, including someone who has never met you.

LiveWhy the voice lever is worth +16 on the ladder4 min▶

The clarity lab climbs 48 → 52 → 68 → 70 → 83 → 98 as each practice is turned on. Two rungs carry half the total climb: voice sample +16 (52 to 68) and cut +15 (83 to 98). Everything else - brief, structure, specifics - adds four points, two points, thirteen points.

That is worth sitting with, because it is not the result most leaders expect. The intuition is that a brief matters most: tell the tool who the reader is and what the ask is, and the writing will land. The brief is worth four points. Handing over three paragraphs of real writing with "match this register" is worth sixteen.

The mechanism is visible in the rows underneath. The corporate default register was carrying most of the passive constructions and about half the AI-tell phrases. Replace the register and both collapse at once, without asking for either. That is why this session comes before the team standard in a5: the voice document is the input that makes every later instruction cheaper.

Two honest caveats. The lab's drafts are written examples, one good version plus one specific kind of damage per missing practice, and the numbers are computed from that text - hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic, a be-verb plus a participle, so read it as a flag rather than a census.

Self-studyThe four things a leader hears instead of "we have no voice document"3 min read▶
  • "Everything sounds a bit generic lately." This is the register complaint. No sample, so the tool supplies the average.
  • "The new starters take forever to sound right." Same root cause, older problem. You were relying on osmosis, and osmosis does not scale past about eight people.
  • "Support and sales sound like the same person now." This is the audience-variant gap. One register for four audiences, because nobody wrote the four down.
  • "I rewrite everything anyway." The most expensive symptom, and the one that gets mistaken for high standards. You are the voice document, held in a single head, applied by hand, at manager rates, on the critical path of every send.

All four are the same missing artifact. That is the useful part: one document, four symptoms, and none of them need a new tool.

Part 2 · the artifact

The two-page voice document, section by section ✎ 18 min build-along

Two pages. Not ten, and not a slide deck. The constraint is the point: a document nobody reads is exactly as useful as a voice nobody wrote down. Four sections, each of which prevents a specific, nameable failure. Draft yours as we go - you will get further in eighteen minutes than you expect, because most of the raw material is already sitting in your sent folder.

LiveSection 1 · three annotated samples, and the annotation is the work4 min▶

Three pieces of writing you would be happy to have sent. Not your best writing ever - your typical good writing, because that is the target. One internal, one customer-facing, one where the news was not great. Real text, real names redacted, roughly two hundred words each.

Then the part people skip: a line under each one saying why it is right. "This opens with the ask in the first eight words." "This says I was wrong without three sentences of preamble." "This uses the customer's own word for the problem instead of ours." Without the annotation you have handed over three artefacts and no theory, and both a new hire and a model will copy the wrong features - the sign-off, the length, the fact that all three happen to start with a name.

Sourcing tip that saves an hour: search your sent folder for the messages that got a fast, clean reply. Fast clean replies are the closest thing you have to a ground-truth label for clarity.

LiveSection 2 · the register rules, in five decisions4 min▶

Five decisions, written as rules that can be broken, which means they can also be followed:

  • Person. Do you write "I" or "we"? When does "you" appear? At Cadence the rule is "I" when a person decided, "we" only when a team genuinely did, and never the royal we to soften bad news.
  • Contractions. Yes or no, and whether that changes for external partners. Pick one. This is the single most visible register signal you have.
  • Sentence length. Not an average, a range and a rhythm rule: "most sentences under twenty words, and vary them deliberately." Generated prose settles into a steady eighteen-to-twenty-two-word hum, and that hum is the most reliable tell there is.
  • How you handle uncertainty. This is the rule that separates honest teams from hedging ones. "Name the confidence and the date you will know more" beats "it is possible that we may potentially".
  • How you say no. Write down the actual shape: the no first, the reason second, the alternative third, and no apology paragraph. Teams that leave this unwritten produce the worst writing in the company, every time.
LiveSection 3 · the vocabulary you use, and the banned list4 min▶

Two short columns. On the left, the words you actually reach for: the plain verb you prefer over its abstract noun, the word your customers use for the thing, the two or three phrases that are genuinely yours.

On the right, the banned list, and this is where the AI-tells go: delve, leverage, seamless, robust, landscape, underscores, it's worth noting, do not hesitate. None of these words is wrong. They are fingerprints. When eight of them appear in a four-paragraph note, the reader forms a view about how much of you was in it, and that view is usually correct.

Build yours from your own last ten documents rather than from my list. Read them and write down every phrase that made you wince slightly. That wince is the most reliable instrument in the room, and it is calibrated to your house rather than mine.

The banned list has a property the rest of the document does not: it is mechanically checkable. Anyone can search a draft for eight strings in fifteen seconds, which means the check survives your absence.

Self-studySection 4 · audience variants without four documents3 min read▶

Four audiences, one page. Say what stays constant and what moves:

  • Internal. Shortest. First names, no context-setting, the ask in the subject line.
  • Customer. Their vocabulary, not yours. No internal team names. Every commitment dated.
  • Exec. The decision first, the three numbers that drive it, then the detail they can skip. Length is a courtesy signal here, not a thoroughness one.
  • External partner. The most formal register you use, and the one where contractions probably go. Also the only place a legal review may be in the path.

What must not vary: honesty about uncertainty, and the fact that a person signed it. If your customer register hedges where your internal register commits, you have not written four variants of one voice. You have written two voices, and only one of them is true.

Open your sent folder and pull three messages that got a fast, clean reply. Do not judge them yet. Fast clean replies are the closest thing you have to a ground-truth label for clarity.

Under each one write one line saying why it is right. If you cannot, it is a sample of habit rather than a sample of voice - swap it out.

Answer the five register decisions: person, contractions, sentence length, how you handle uncertainty, how you say no. Write them as rules that could be broken.

Read your last ten documents and list every phrase that made you wince. Cap the banned list at twelve entries so it stays searchable in fifteen seconds.

Add the audience variants last, in four short paragraphs, and say explicitly what does not change between them.

Adjective brief - unmatchable

"Draft a note to the education customers about the proposal slipping. Use a professional, friendly, human tone that reflects our brand. Keep it warm but authoritative, and make sure it sounds like us."

Sample brief - matchable, and breakable

"Match the register of the three paragraphs below, which I wrote. [three real paragraphs pasted in full] Rules: first person singular, contractions on, most sentences under 20 words, name the confidence and the date rather than hedging, and if there is a no, put it in the first sentence. Never use these words: delve, leverage, seamless, robust, landscape, underscores, it's worth noting, do not hesitate. Now draft a note to the education customers about the proposal slipping to Thursday. 120 words."

Real world

The Cadence week, in one comparison. Three teams briefed the same tool the same Monday. Support asked for "friendly and human", product asked for "clear and modern", finance asked for "professional". By Wednesday a customer who had threads open with all three said the company had "clearly centralised comms" - a compliment that was actually a diagnosis. Nothing had been centralised. Three different people had handed the same tool three adjectives, and the tool had resolved all three to its own default register. The fix that shipped Friday was not a policy. It was three sets of samples and one shared banned list.

Part 3 · why models flatten voice

Convergence is the default, and there is evidence 8 min live

You do not have to take the flattening on faith, and you should not present it to your own leadership as a hunch. Two studies make the point cleanly, and the second one closes the escape hatch the first one leaves open.

Meincke, Mollick & Terwiesch, "Prompting Diverse Ideas," arXiv:2402.01727 measured cosine similarity within pools of ideas, where lower means more diverse. A group of students scored 0.243. Chain-of-Thought prompting scored 0.255. A base GPT-4 prompt scored 0.377. Read that as two findings at once: a default model pool is markedly more internally similar than a human crowd, and better prompting closes most of the gap. The second half is the hopeful part, and it is the reason a voice document works at all - direction genuinely moves the number.

The obvious next thought is "then we will use a different model". Wenger & Kenett, "Large language models are homogeneously creative," PNAS Nexus 5(3), pgag042, 24 March 2026 tested 102 humans against 22 LLMs across three divergent-thinking tasks. The LLMs showed markedly lower semantic distance, with effect sizes of 0.87 to 1.8, and it held across model families. So switching vendors does not fix it. This is a property of the class of tool, not a quirk of one product.

The leader conclusion is short. Convergence is the default state, not an accident, and a written voice is the only cheap defence. You cannot procure your way out of it, you cannot wait for the next model to solve it, and you cannot get there with adjectives. Two pages and three samples is the intervention that is actually available to you this week.

Self-studyWhat these studies do not say3 min read▶

Use them precisely or they will be used against you in the next meeting:

  • They measured idea and response pools, not your team's emails. The mechanism transfers, and I am arguing from mechanism when I extend it to writing. Say that out loud rather than implying the papers measured Cadence.
  • Cosine similarity is not a quality score. 0.377 versus 0.243 says "more internally similar", not "worse". Homogeneity is the problem here because voice is the thing you are protecting.
  • The Meincke result is partly good news. Chain-of-Thought at 0.255 lands near the student group's 0.243. Direction works. That is the entire premise of the artifact you just drafted.
  • Effect sizes of 0.87 to 1.8 are large, not universal. Across model families, across three tasks. It is strong evidence that vendor-switching is not a remedy, not proof that every model output is identical.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 90-120 min total

  • Draft the two pages. Four sections, hard two-page limit. Timebox it to forty minutes and accept that version one is rough - a rough written voice beats a polished remembered one.
  • Collect three samples. Search your sent folder for messages that got a fast, clean reply. Pick one internal, one customer-facing, one where the news was bad. Write the "why this is right" line under each, and treat that line as the deliverable.
  • Write the banned list from your own last ten documents. Read them, note every phrase that makes you wince, and keep the list under twelve entries so it stays checkable in fifteen seconds.
  • Test it on someone else. Hand the document to a colleague who did not write it, have them brief a model with it on a real task, and read the output cold. Anything you have to explain afterwards is a gap in the document, not in the colleague.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why is "professional, friendly, human" a useless voice brief?

An instruction that cannot be violated is not an instruction. Nobody can hold a draft against three adjectives and say no, and five people on your team will each read "professional" differently. Samples can be matched, rules can be broken, and a banned list can be searched - all three are checkable, which is exactly what adjectives are not.

2 · On the clarity lab ladder, which lever is worth +16 points, tied with cutting as the biggest single jump?

The ladder runs 48 → 52 → 68 → 70 → 83 → 98. The voice sample takes 52 to 68, and cutting takes 83 to 98. The brief is worth four points, structure two. The corporate default register was carrying most of the passives and about half the AI-tells, so replacing the register collapses both without asking for either.

3 · Cosine similarity, lower being more diverse: students 0.243, Chain-of-Thought 0.255, base GPT-4 prompt 0.377. What does the pair of numbers 0.255 and 0.377 tell a leader?

Similarity is not a quality measure, so B overreads it. C is contradicted by Wenger & Kenett: 102 humans against 22 LLMs, effect sizes 0.87 to 1.8, holding across model families. The useful reading is A - the default pool is markedly more internally similar than a human crowd, and direction moves the number most of the way back.
