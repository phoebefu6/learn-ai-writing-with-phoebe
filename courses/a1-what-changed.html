What changed in how work gets written

You have been approving writing for years, and the thing you were trained to catch has almost disappeared. Clumsy prose is close to extinct in your inbox. What replaced it is worse for you specifically: writing that is warm, grammatical, correctly structured and carries nothing you can act on. It passes review because an absence is not an error you can circle. This session gives you the picture that the rest of the leader track hangs on, an honest account of which parts of the writing week actually got faster, and three things your team quietly loses if nobody names the shift out loud.

Part 1 · the spine of the track

The failure mode changed 8 min live

Every reviewing instinct you have was trained on writing that looks wrong. Tangled sentences, a paragraph that never lands, a typo in the customer's name. That category is nearly extinct, and you should be glad: it was genuinely bad and it is genuinely gone. What arrived in its place is prose that is fluent, courteous, well-organised and empty. It does not trip any of your alarms, because your alarms are wired to errors, and an absence is not an error. Put fluency on one axis and substance on the other and the whole problem becomes a picture you can point at in a meeting.

LiveWhy the empty quadrant survives your review4 min▶

Reviewing is a pattern-matching job, and every pattern you hold is a positive one: something is present that should not be. A comma splice. A number that contradicts the table. A tone that will land badly with the client. Fluent emptiness offers you none of those. Read it again and it stays reasonable. Read it a third time and you start to suspect yourself.

  • There is nothing to circle. You can put a line under a tangled sentence. You cannot put a line under a missing number, because there is nowhere to put it.
  • Politeness does a lot of work. A draft that thanks the reader, acknowledges the complexity and promises to follow up feels considerate rather than evasive.
  • Length reads as effort. Two hundred careful words look like someone tried. One vague sentence looks lazy. The two contain the same amount of information.
  • The cost lands on someone else. The reader burns ten minutes and still has to ask. That cost never shows up in your review, so it never gets attributed to the draft.

This is why a2 exists. The whole review standard is a device for turning an absence into something nameable, because a name is the only form a reviewer can act on and the only form a writer can fix.

Self-studyThe sentence to stop saying in reviews3 min read▶

"It feels a bit long." You have said it this month. It is almost always true and almost always the wrong diagnosis, and it teaches the writer to trim adjectives instead of going to find a fact.

A piece that says nothing has to keep talking. It has no facts to anchor it, so it circles, restates its opening in its closing paragraph, and pads the middle with the kind of sentence that could appear in any document about anything. Ask for it to be shorter and you get the same emptiness at 60% scale. Ask what the reader is supposed to decide, and the padding loses its excuse.

Two better sentences, both of which you will practise in a2: "I cannot find the ask" and "two of these nine sentences carry a number." Neither is a style opinion. Both are checkable in front of the writer, which means neither turns into a debate about taste.

Part 2 · what actually got faster

Where the week's writing time goes, and what moved 7 min live

Here is the honest version, and I want to be explicit about how I am arguing it: this is an argument from mechanism, not from a survey. I have no trustworthy figure for how much of your team's writing is now model-assisted, so I am not going to invent one, and you should be suspicious of anyone who quotes you a tidy percentage. What I can argue is structural. Writing at work is five steps. Exactly one of them is a text-production problem, and a text generator collapsed it from twenty minutes to four seconds. The other four are decision problems, judgement problems and accountability problems, and none of them got a tool.

LiveWhat "we got faster at writing" actually means4 min▶

Two things are true at once, and a manager who only holds one of them will make a bad call.

  • Step 3 really did collapse. Nobody should be nostalgic about staring at a blank page for forty minutes. Blank-page paralysis was a real tax and it is largely gone. That is a genuine win and you should say so out loud, or your team will assume you are anti-tool and stop telling you how they work.
  • The queue moved rather than shortened. If drafting cost most of the effort and drafting is now free, then the binding constraint is whatever is next in line - and what is next in line is cutting, verifying, and someone deciding they are willing to put their name on it. Those are the steps that show up in your calendar.

The failure mode for leaders is to bank the step-3 saving as a headcount or timeline argument, then act surprised when review time goes up. Volume rises because producing text is cheap. Each document costs more to approve because more of them arrive with nothing decided. That is not a tooling problem and no upgrade fixes it.

Self-studyThe Thursday decision at Cadence3 min read▶

Here is the case in one paragraph, because it recurs in every session. The education proposal has slipped. New evidence from the field suggests the real buyer is not the head of department you have been writing to but the procurement lead who has never heard of you. One technical assumption underneath the pricing was never tested. A decision is needed on Thursday.

Four people send you writing about this during the week. All four documents are fluent. Two of them do not say what they want you to decide. One of them describes the untested assumption as "an area we are continuing to explore," which is a sentence with a person hiding inside it. None of them are badly written by the standards you were trained on.

Notice what your job is in that week: not improving prose. Working out, fast, which of these four documents contains a decision and which three contain a description of a situation. a2 gives you the seven-check pass that does that in about a minute.

Part 3 · the quiet costs

Three things you lose if nobody names the shift 10 min live · open all three

None of these announce themselves. There is no incident, no angry customer, no dashboard turning red. They are all slow, and two of them have real evidence behind them that I want you to be able to cite accurately, because the version people repeat in meetings is usually wrong.

LiveLoss 1 · Your reviewers stop catching anything3 min▶

A review step survives in an organisation because it catches things. When the thing it was built to catch stops arriving, the step does not get deleted - it keeps running and starts producing comments about commas.

Watch for the signature: review threads where every comment is about wording, none is about whether the document has an ask, and the piece ships on time with everyone reasonably satisfied. That is a review process that has quietly become a ritual. Your reviewers are not lazy. They are doing exactly what they were trained to do, on a problem that no longer exists.

The fix is not more review. It is a different check, applied earlier, that names absences rather than errors - and a rule about which absences send a draft back instead of getting fixed in the margin. That is a2, and it is the highest-leverage forty-five minutes in this track.

LiveLoss 2 · Junior writers stop developing judgement4 min▶

Drafting from scratch is unpleasant, slow, and the main way anyone learns to think in prose. If your least experienced writers never do it, they get fluent output on day one and stop climbing. You will not notice for a year, and then you will notice all at once, when someone needs to write something no model can help with.

There is direct evidence here. Anderson, Shah & Kreminski, "Homogenization Effects of Large Language Models on Human Creative Ideation," C&C 2024 (DOI 10.1145/3635636.3656204) ran 36 participants: with ChatGPT, different users produced less semantically distinct ideas than with another creativity-support tool, while producing more of them - and they felt less ownership of those ideas.

For a writing course, the ownership finding is the one that matters. More output, less distinct, and the person does not feel it is theirs. Ownership is not a soft outcome in writing - it is the thing that makes someone check a number twice, argue with a stakeholder about a claim, and refuse to send a paragraph they cannot defend. A writer who does not feel ownership will not fight for a sentence, and step 5 of the loop is exactly that fight.

What to do about it is a management choice, not a policy: decide which pieces are training pieces and say so explicitly. a6 goes at protecting drafting time properly.

LiveLoss 3 · Everything starts to sound the same3 min▶

The third loss is the one you feel before you can name it: your team's writing converges. Not on something bad, exactly - on one competent, agreeable, mid-register voice. A team whose documents are indistinguishable from each other has lost information, because register carries meaning. You could once tell from two sentences whether an update came from the engineer who understates everything or the account lead who does not.

The evidence to cite carefully: Doshi & Hauser, "Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content," Science Advances 10(28), eadn5290, 12 July 2024 - 293 writers, 600 evaluators, 3,519 evaluations. Individual story novelty rose 5.4% with one AI idea and 8.1% with five. The least creative writers gained the most (novelty +10.7%, usefulness +11.5% in the five-idea condition). And stories became measurably more similar to one another within a condition.

Say that last part exactly that way. The paper's similarity figures are expressed as a share of the total range of a similarity coefficient, so "stories were 10.7% more similar" is not a claim the paper makes and quoting it that way will get you corrected by the one person in the room who read it. "Measurably more similar to one another, per Doshi and Hauser" is both accurate and sufficient.

The management reading is genuinely two-sided: your weakest writers get better, and your collective range narrows. If you only manage the average you will be delighted. a3 is how you keep the range - a house voice captured as samples rather than adjectives.

Self-studyWhat to say to the team on Monday3 min read▶

You cannot manage a shift nobody has named, and the naming has to come from you or it arrives as a rumour that you are anti-tool. Four sentences, in this order, are enough:

  • Say the win first. Drafting got cheap and staring at a blank page is not a skill anyone should have to demonstrate. If you skip this, everything after it reads as disapproval and people stop telling you how they actually work.
  • Say what did not get cheaper. Deciding what a document is for, cutting it, and being willing to put your name on it. Those are the same as they were and they are still the job.
  • Say what you will now ask for. The ask, up front, and the numbers. Not a tool policy, not a disclosure form - two questions you will ask about every document from now on. That is a2 in one line.
  • Say what you will not ask. You are not going to ask whether a model helped, because you cannot tell and it is the wrong question. Saying this out loud removes a fear people are carrying quietly and will not raise.

a5 turns those four sentences into a one-page standard you publish. Tonight the point is smaller: the shift is real, it is nobody's fault, and it is now a named thing your team is allowed to talk about.

Real world

People do not check the output, and we have a number for it. The Nielsen Norman Group ran a two-week diary study on generative AI use, published 24 September 2023: 18 participants, 425 logged conversations. Only 22.43% of those conversations were followed by any verification at all. Read that as a manager rather than as a scold. It is not that your people are careless - it is that fluent output does not feel like it needs checking, in the same way a confident colleague does not feel like they need checking. Verification is a habit that has to be designed into a process, because nothing in the reading experience will prompt it. a4 goes at over-reliance properly, and a5 turns it into a line in the team standard.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Pull the last three documents you approved. Put each one in a quadrant, and be honest about the fluent-and-empty box. Bring the count to a2.
  • For each of the three, try to write the ask in one sentence without reopening the document. If you cannot, that is the finding, and it is about the document rather than your memory.
  • Count specifics on one of them: how many sentences carry a number or a name? Divide by the sentence count. Under a third and the risks in that document are reading as feelings.
  • Ask one junior writer how they drafted their last piece. Do not evaluate, do not hint, just record what they say. This is your baseline for the ownership question in Loss 2, and you cannot get it later.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which quadrant is your existing review process least likely to catch?

Clumsy work is visible, which makes it safe: it gets sent back. Fluent emptiness reads as considerate and thorough, offers nothing to point at, and ships. That is why the rest of the track is about naming absences rather than finding errors.

2 · Drafting collapsed in cost. Which parts of the writing loop did not compress?

Only step 3 is a text-production problem. Deciding is a judgement problem, cutting is a taste-and-priority problem, owning is an accountability problem. Making the cheap step cheaper moves the queue rather than shortening the week - which is why your review time goes up rather than down.

3 · In Anderson, Shah & Kreminski (C&C 2024), which finding matters most for a writing team?

They produced more ideas, not fewer - less distinct, and with less ownership. Ownership is the one to hold onto: it is what makes a writer check a number twice and defend a sentence. Option C is the misquote to avoid, and it belongs to a different paper anyway.
