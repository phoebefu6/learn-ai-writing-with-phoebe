Difficult writing: mostly the wrong tool

Every other session in this track has been about getting more out of the tool. This one argues the opposite, and argues it plainly: for bad news, pushback, hard feedback, refusals and apologies, AI drafting is usually the wrong tool rather than a risky one. That is a strong claim and it has to be earned, so we will do it from mechanism - what these five genres actually require, and why a system with no stake in the relationship and a construction-level habit of hedging cannot supply it. Then the honest other half: the four places a model genuinely helps with hard writing, because a purist rule would be wrong too.

Part 0

Where this sits in the track

In b1 you wrote a skip rule: three kinds of writing you would not hand to a model. Tonight we test whether it survives, and most people's rule turns out to be either too vague to use or too strict to be honest. The writing in this session is a small share of your week and a large share of how you are remembered - the message about someone's project, someone's performance, someone's request. It is also the writing people most want to delegate, precisely because it is uncomfortable, which is exactly the wrong reason to reach for a tool.

Part 1 · what the reader is actually doing

Five hard genres, and what each one requires 8 min live

These five cover almost all of it, and each has a different requirement, which is why "be tactful" is useless advice. Notice what is not on the list of requirements: warmth, polish, fluency. All five readers are doing the same underlying thing - checking whether a person thought about them specifically - and they are unusually alert while they do it, because the message concerns their work, their standing or their time.

LiveThe one sentence each genre must contain4 min▶

Each of these has a load-bearing sentence. If it is missing, nothing else in the message matters, and the most common failure is that the sentence is present but softened until it no longer says the thing:

GenreThe sentence that must existHow it usually fails
Bad newsThe thing that happened, in the first line, with the dateThree paragraphs of context first, so the reader skims and panics
Pushback"I do not think we should do X, because Y"Turned into a question so nobody has to disagree with anyone
Hard feedback"In the 6 March review you did X, and the effect was Y"Becomes a character judgement, or a compliment sandwich with no filling
Saying no"No, and here is what I can do instead""Let me see what I can do" - a false door, and a week of someone's hope
Apology"I got X wrong. Here is what I am doing about it"An explanation that is secretly an excuse, usually signalled by "but"

Write that sentence first, by hand, before anything else. If you cannot write it, you have not decided yet - and that is step 1 of the loop, not a drafting problem.

Self-studyWhy these five and not "sensitive communications"3 min read▶

Grouping them as "sensitive" is what produces the useless advice, because it suggests a single move - be gentler - when the five requirements pull in different directions. Bad news wants speed. Pushback wants a claim you can attack. Feedback wants precision about behaviour and silence about character. A refusal wants finality. An apology wants ownership and nothing else, especially not context.

Being gentler helps with exactly none of these, and actively damages three: gentleness in bad news becomes burying, gentleness in a refusal becomes a false maybe, and gentleness in an apology becomes the "but" that undoes it. The category "difficult" is about how it feels to write, not about what the writing needs. Sort by requirement and each one becomes a short, specific job.

Part 2 · the argument, from mechanism

Why a model is a poor fit here 8 min live

I am not going to argue this from sentiment, because "some things should be human" is a preference, not a reason, and it loses the moment someone shows you a well-drafted apology. Here are three mechanisms. Each one is a property of how the tool works rather than a claim about souls, and each one maps onto something you have already watched happen in the lab.

LiveOne: the content IS the relationship, and it has no stake in yours3 min▶

In a status update, the words are a container for information and the container is close to interchangeable. In these five genres the words are the substance. When you tell someone their project is being cut, the information takes one sentence; everything else the message does is demonstrate how much thought you gave to their position before you pressed send.

A model has no position on any of that. It has no stake in whether this person still trusts you in six months, so it optimises for the only thing it can see: producing text that reads as appropriate. Appropriate text in this genre means smooth. And smoothness is precisely the wrong output, because the reader is not looking for smooth - they are looking for evidence that you thought about them, which shows up as specificity about their situation. The model cannot supply that, because it does not know it, and if you brief it well enough to supply it you have already done the thinking that was the hard part.

That is the real cost. The work you were trying to delegate is the deciding, and deciding is step 1 of the loop, where the tool never helped.

LiveTwo: it hedges by construction, and hedging here reads as evasion3 min▶

You have watched this happen already. In the lab, press "sound more professional" on a finished draft and it costs 36 points and adds 42 words: "I need a decision" becomes "it would be appreciated if we could obtain a decision". That is what a model does to a hard sentence when it feels pressure to be careful - and a difficult message is nothing but pressure to be careful.

In an update, a hedge costs the reader half a second. In a difficult message it changes the meaning of the message. Consider what the reader concludes from each version:

  • "We are not funding the pilot next quarter" - a decision, delivered by a person who made it.
  • "It seems likely that funding for the pilot may need to be revisited in the coming period" - the reader now believes there is room to negotiate, that nobody has decided, and that you are managing them. All three are worse than the bad news.

Softness reads as kindness only to the writer. To the reader it reads as evasion, and evasion in a message about their work is what they will remember, long after they have forgotten the decision itself.

LiveThree: the reader is unusually attentive, so the tells are conspicuous3 min▶

The seven measures include rhythm - the spread of sentence lengths - and the course's claim is that a steady 18-to-22-word hum is the most reliable tell of generated prose. In a Tuesday status update nobody notices, because nobody is reading closely. They are scanning for the one line that concerns them.

A message about someone's job is read differently. It is read twice, then read out loud to a partner, then screenshotted to a colleague. At that level of attention the flat rhythm, the balanced three-part sentences and the stock phrases stop being invisible. "I want to acknowledge how difficult this must be" is a perfectly kind sentence that has been sent by ten thousand people this year, and the recipient of a hard message has an unusual ear for whether the words were assembled or chosen.

You do not need a detector for this, and you should not claim anyone can reliably identify AI writing - b2 in the leader track says so explicitly. The risk is not that they will identify a tool. It is that they will conclude you did not spend yourself on it, which is a judgement people make on feel and rarely say out loud.

Self-studyThe evidence that bears directly on this3 min read▶

There is one study in this course's source list that speaks to difficult writing specifically, and it is worth stating exactly and not extending:

Anderson, Shah & Kreminski, "Homogenization Effects of Large Language Models on Human Creative Ideation," C&C 2024, DOI 10.1145/3635636.3656204. 36 participants: different users produced less semantically distinct ideas with ChatGPT than with another creativity-support tool, while producing more of them, and felt less ownership of them.

The ownership finding is the one that matters here. It is a study of creative ideation, not of apologies, so do not stretch it - it does not show that AI-assisted apologies land worse. What it does show is that people who worked with a model felt less like the output was theirs. Now hold that against what the five genres require. Felt ownership is not a nice side effect in a difficult message, it is the payload: you are telling someone that you decided this, you are accountable for it, you thought about them. A process that reliably reduces your sense of ownership is a strange choice for the one message where ownership is the entire point.

Note also the second half of the finding: more ideas, less distinct. That is the shape of the whole tool. Volume up, distinctiveness down. In most writing that trade is fine. Here, distinctiveness is what the reader is checking for.

Part 3 · the honest other half

Where a model does help with hard writing 7 min live

A purist rule would be wrong, and it would also not survive contact with a hard week. The pattern is consistent across all four uses below: the model is useful as a reader, never as the writer. You write the message. It tells you how the message will land, what you have hidden in it, and what comes back at you. That is real help, and it is help at exactly the step where you are least able to see clearly, because you are anxious.

LiveStress-test it as the recipient on their worst day2 min▶

The most valuable of the four. You are writing to the reader you imagine; they will read it in whatever mood they are actually in.

Use this one

"You are the recipient of this message, reading it on your worst day - you slept badly, you already think this project is being wound down, and you are looking for evidence you are being managed out. List every sentence you would misread, and what you would take it to mean. Do not rewrite anything."

The "do not rewrite anything" clause matters. Without it you get a softened draft back and lose the diagnosis. What you want is a list, and typically two items on it are real - a sentence that looks like a hint, and an absence the reader will fill in with the worst option.

LiveCheck you have not buried the lede2 min▶

You cannot check this on your own draft, because you know what it says. A model reading cold is genuinely useful here.

Use this one

"Reading only the first two sentences of this message, what is the news? Quote the sentence where the actual news appears, and count how many words come before it."

The word count is the whole point. If the answer is "the news is in sentence four, 71 words in", you have your edit and you did not need an opinion to get it. This one is mechanical, which is why it is reliable - you are asking for a location, not a judgement.

LiveFind the sentence that is secretly an excuse2 min▶

Apologies and slipped-deadline messages fail here more than anywhere. The writer believes they are supplying context. The reader hears blame being distributed.

Use this one

"Find every sentence in this that explains why something happened. For each one, say whether a sceptical reader would hear accountability or an excuse, and name who it quietly blames. Then flag any sentence containing "but", "however" or "unfortunately"."

The three flagged words are where excuses live. You will not always cut them, but you should have to defend each one.

LiveRehearse the reply you are dreading2 min▶

Half of the difficulty in hard writing is not the message, it is the response you are bracing for. Rehearsing it costs three minutes and removes most of the dread, which is usually what has kept the message in drafts since Tuesday.

Use this one

"Write the three replies I am most afraid of receiving to this, in the recipient's likely register: the angry one, the wounded one, and the one that escalates to my manager. For each, tell me which sentence of mine it is reacting to."

The third column is the useful one. Often the sentence provoking the worst imagined reply is a hedge you added to be kind, and deleting it removes the ambiguity that the angry reply was built on.

LiveThe two request shapes, side by side3 min▶

First, what drafting produces versus what you would have written yourself. Read both aloud - the difference is not quality, it is presence:

Model-drafted bad news · fluent, warm, evasive

"Hi Maya - I hope this finds you well. I wanted to reach out and share some thoughts following our recent discussions regarding the education pilot. As you know, we have been carefully evaluating our priorities for the coming period, and it seems likely that the timeline may need to be revisited somewhat. I want to acknowledge how much effort the team has put in, and to say that this in no way reflects on the quality of that work. Please do not hesitate to reach out if you would like to discuss further."

Written by you · 61 words, and answerable

"Maya - the education pilot is not going ahead this quarter. Two of the three schools moved their budget cycle to September, so the case we built in January no longer holds. That is my call and I made it on Tuesday. Your team's work is not the reason and I have said so to Ravi in writing. I would like fifteen minutes on Thursday to talk about where the three of you go next - I have two options and a preference."

Now the request shape, which matters more than the draft:

Makes it worse and feels like progress

"Can you soften this so it does not sound so harsh?"

Tells you something you did not know

"Tell me where this will be misread. Quote each sentence, say what the reader will take it to mean, and say what they will do next as a result."

"Soften" gets you hedges, and hedges are the thing this genre cannot afford. "Where will this be misread" gets you a list. One request improves the tone and damages the message; the other leaves you to write every word and makes the message land.

Part 4 · memorise these

Four sentence patterns worth knowing by heart 5 min live

Difficult writing is not improved by having more options. It is improved by having four patterns so worn-in that you can produce them while your heart rate is up. These are the ones I would put on a card.

LiveThe one-line lede for bad news1 min▶

Pattern: "[Name] - [the thing that happened], [when]." Then stop. Then the reason, in one sentence. Then what happens next.

"Maya - the education pilot is not going ahead this quarter." Twelve words, no windup, and the reader's uncertainty ends in the first four seconds instead of building through three paragraphs of context. Everything you wanted to say about effort and appreciation still gets said. It gets said after, where it reads as respect rather than as cushioning, and where the reader can actually hear it because they are no longer scanning for the news.

Live"Here is what I would do instead" - the refusal that contributes1 min▶

Pattern: "No, I cannot [the specific ask]. What I can do is [something real and smaller]. If that does not work, [name the person or route that might]."

This is the difference between a no that costs you goodwill and a no that spends none. Note what it does not contain: an apology for existing, an explanation of your workload, or "let me see what I can do". That last phrase is the false door, and it is the single most expensive kindness in professional writing - it converts a clean no into a week of someone else's hope, and then you have to say no twice.

LiveThe behavioural feedback frame1 min▶

Pattern: "In [specific situation], you [specific observable thing]. The effect was [specific consequence]. What I need next time is [specific behaviour]."

Four slots, all of them concrete, and the person is nowhere in the sentence - only their actions. "In the 6 March review you answered the finance question with an estimate you had not checked. Ravi built his forecast on it. What I need next time is either the checked number or the words 'I will confirm by Friday'."

Compare "you can be a bit overconfident with numbers", which is a claim about who someone is, cannot be acted on, and cannot be argued with. This is the same specifics discipline the lab measures, applied where it is hardest to hold: under pressure, about a person, in a conversation you did not want to have.

LiveThe apology with no "but" in it1 min▶

Pattern: "I got [the specific thing] wrong. [What it cost, named plainly.] Here is what I am doing about it: [action, with a date]." No "but". No "however". No paragraph about the circumstances.

"I told the board the integration was tested. It was not, and they made a hiring decision on that. I have written to them today with the correct position, and from now on I will not report a status I have not seen myself."

The test is mechanical: search the draft for "but", "however", "unfortunately" and "to be fair". Every hit is a candidate excuse. The context you are itching to supply is real, and it belongs in a different message on a different day, because in this one it will be heard as a bid to reduce your share of the blame - which, if you are honest, is often exactly what it is.

Build-along

Two rows only, on a message you have actually sent ★ 14 min · everyone runs it

Paste in a difficult message you really sent, or the one you have been avoiding since Tuesday. Then read two rows only: hedges and specifics. Ignore the total. Difficult writing legitimately breaks some of the measures - short paragraphs wreck the rhythm score, a plain one-line lede reads as a low grade level, and an honest statement of uncertainty is counted as a hedge because the tool cannot tell honesty from evasion. A low overall score here is not the point and is not a problem.

Paste the message. Read the hedge count first and go through the list one at a time, asking of each: is this carrying information about my confidence, or covering the fact that I did not want to commit?

Read the specifics row. In a difficult message, a name or a date in a sentence is usually evidence that you thought about this person's actual situation. Vagueness here is not a style issue, it is the reader's cue that this was generic.

Delete the false door. Search your own text for "let me see what I can do", "we may need to revisit", "it seems likely" and "I want to acknowledge". Delete or commit, one at a time, watching the hedge row fall.

Now find your lede. Count the words before the actual news. If it is more than about fifteen, cut everything above it and paste it back underneath.

Ignore the total score, deliberately, and note what it says anyway. Then write down the one sentence in your message that a reader could argue with. If there is not one, you wrote around the thing.

The scorecard is the wrong judge for this session, on purpose Use two rows and no more: hedges and specifics. A difficult message will often score badly overall and be exactly right - short sentences flatten the rhythm measure, a plain lede lowers the reading grade, and honest uncertainty is counted as hedging because no measurement can tell "we do not know yet" from "I would rather not say". Nothing you paste leaves your browser. Every number is computed from the text you typed: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic, so read it as a flag rather than a census - and here, read the total the same way.
Real world

The tell is almost never the prose. It is the missing detail. When someone forwards you a message and says "this feels off", ask them to point at what is wrong and they usually cannot - the sentences are fine. What is missing is any fact that could only have come from thinking about them: the date of the meeting, the name of the person who decided, the thing they said in January that you remembered. A model cannot put those in because it does not know them, and the moment you gather them you have done the work you were trying to avoid. That is the whole session in one line: in difficult writing, the part you want to delegate is the part that is the message.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Write the message you have been avoiding, by hand, using the relevant pattern from Part 4. Then run the stress-test prompt on it and fix only the sentences it says will be misread. Send it. The sending is the homework.
  • Go back to the skip rule you wrote in b1 and rewrite it now that you have the mechanism. Test it against a real case where you would be tempted: two versions of the same hard email, one you drafted and one a model drafted, read a day later.
  • Take one message you have already sent and run the buried-lede check on it: how many words came before the news? Rewrite the opening line and keep both versions for b10's capstone.
  • Find a "but", "however" or "unfortunately" in something you sent recently and work out what it was protecting you from. This is uncomfortable and it is the fastest single improvement available in this session.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why does hedging read as evasion in a difficult message when it barely registers in a status update?

In an update a hedge costs half a second. "We are not funding the pilot next quarter" becoming "it seems likely that funding may need to be revisited" tells the reader three false things: that it is not settled, that they can push, and that you are managing them. Softness reads as kindness to the writer and as evasion to the reader, which is why the lab's anti-lever costs 36 points and adds 42 words.

2 · What is the consistent shape of the four places a model genuinely helps with hard writing?

You write every word; it tells you how the words will land. Each of the four asks for a location or a list rather than a rewrite, which is why "do not rewrite anything" belongs in the stress-test prompt. Ask it to soften and you get hedges, which is the one thing this genre cannot afford.

3 · Anderson, Shah and Kreminski (C&C 2024) studied 36 participants. What did they find, and why does it matter here?

The study covers creative ideation, not apologies, so do not stretch it - it found more ideas, less distinct, and less felt ownership. That last part is what bears on this session: a difficult message exists to say that you decided, you are accountable, you thought about them. Reducing your own sense of ownership is a strange trade for the one message where ownership is the whole point.
