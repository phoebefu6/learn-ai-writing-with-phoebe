The brief for a piece of writing

A brief is five slots long and takes about ninety seconds to fill: reader, decision, ask, facts, register. Skip it and you get a draft that opens with a windup and closes without a request. Fill four of the five and you get something worse, because a model that has no facts does not leave a blank - it writes "a fair amount" and "considerably longer than anticipated", and those read as information. Tonight we build the brief, watch the lab prove that brief without facts is only worth 4 points, and learn the habit that separates people who get value from a model from people who argue with one: when the draft is wrong, change the brief.

Part 1 · the artifact

Five slots, and a test for each one 14 min live

The brief is not a prompt. It is the thinking you were going to have to do anyway, written down in an order that happens to be readable by a model - and by a colleague, and by you in three weeks when you have forgotten why you sent the thing. Each slot has a one-line test for whether it is specific enough, and every one of those tests fails in the same direction: too abstract to act on. The Cadence week is the running example. The education proposal has slipped two weeks, 48 customer samples have changed who the buyer is, one technical assumption about integration is untested, and the exec team needs to decide on Thursday.

LiveSlot 1 · Reader - who exactly, and what do they already know3 min▶

The test: could you name the person, and one thing they already believe that you are about to change?

"The exec team" is not a reader. "Our CFO, who signed off the education plan in March on a six-month payback and has not seen the 48 samples" is a reader, and it determines almost everything downstream: how much context you owe, what you can leave out, and which fact lands hardest. Half the length problem in work writing is a reader problem - when you do not know what they know, you explain everything, just in case.

The second half of the slot is the one people skip. Write down what they already know. That list is your permission to delete. In the Cadence case the exec team already knows the proposal exists and already knows the timeline; what they do not know is that the evidence has moved the buyer from an IT budget to an academic one, which is the only genuinely new thing in the piece and belongs in the first sentence.

LiveSlot 2 · Decision - what changes because they read this3 min▶

The test: can you finish this sentence without using the word "aware"? "After reading this, they will ______."

If the honest answer is "be aware of the situation", you are writing theatre and you should either find the real decision or not send the piece. Awareness is what we write when we want credit for having communicated without asking anyone for anything. It is also the single most reliable predictor of a draft nobody replies to.

At Cadence the decision is concrete and slightly uncomfortable: either the proposal ships to academic buyers on the original date with the integration assumption untested, or it slips a further four weeks while engineering settles it. That is a real fork, and naming it is what makes the writing worth reading. Note how much shorter the piece gets once the decision is on the page - you no longer need three paragraphs of setup, because the fork explains itself.

LiveSlot 3 · Ask - the single action, with a date2 min▶

The test: is it one action, is there a date, and could the reader do it today without asking you a question first?

Three failures show up here constantly. Plural asks: a list of four requests, which reliably produces zero. Undated asks: "when you get a chance", which is a promise that nothing will happen. Asks that are really questions about the ask: "let me know your thoughts", which hands the reader the job of working out what you needed.

The Cadence ask is one sentence: "Reply by Thursday with ship or slip." That is a date, an action, and a binary. It also does something for you that is easy to miss - it makes the rest of the writing accountable, because every paragraph now has to earn its place by helping the reader choose one of two words.

LiveSlot 4 · Facts - the numbers, names and dates you supply3 min▶

The test: could a reader check every claim in the piece against something that exists?

This is the slot that costs you real time, and the only one you cannot delegate. In the Cadence brief it is five things: the proposal has slipped two weeks, the decision is due Thursday, the academic procurement cycle runs 6 to 12 months, 31 institutions are in the current pipeline, and 48 customer samples moved the buyer. Five facts, four of which were sitting in a spreadsheet someone already owned.

Now the honest part, and it is the most useful sentence in this session: if you cannot fill this slot, the writing is not ready. Not "the prompt needs work" - the work needs work. The instinct to draft anyway is strong precisely because drafting feels like progress, and a model will happily give you 200 words that look like progress. What you actually needed was twenty minutes and a query. In b6 we come back to this as a send-back rule: a draft with no checkable claims goes back to the author, not to the editor.

Self-studySlot 5 · Register - the voice sample, not the adjective3 min read▶

The test: is there real writing pasted into the slot, or only words describing writing?

"Friendly but professional", "warm and confident", "clear and concise" - all of these are compatible with the corporate default, which is why they change almost nothing. A model cannot infer your register from an adjective any more than a tailor can infer your measurements from "smart casual". What works is three to eight paragraphs of your own sent writing plus an instruction to match it, and a short banned list.

Tonight you can fill this slot roughly: paste something you actually sent. b3 builds it properly, because the register lever is worth +16 points on the ladder, the biggest single jump there is, and it deserves its own session rather than a bullet at the end of this one.

Part 2 · the central idea

Plausible vagueness 7 min live

Here is the mechanism, because once you see it you cannot unsee it. You ask for an update and you do not supply the numbers. The model needs a sentence in that position. It cannot leave a gap, because a gap is not a fluent continuation, so it produces the most probable thing that fits the shape of an update: "the timeline has extended considerably longer than anticipated", "early research suggests a shift in buyer profile", "a fair amount of pipeline is affected". Every one of those is grammatical, appropriately cautious, and completely unfalsifiable. And it reads as information.

Real world

The five Cadence facts, and what fills the hole when you withhold them. "Slipped two weeks" becomes "the timeline has moved out somewhat". "Decision needed Thursday" becomes "we would appreciate direction in the near term". "6 to 12 months" becomes "procurement cycles in this segment can be lengthy". "31 institutions" becomes "a number of institutions". "48 samples" becomes "some early customer research". Read the right-hand column as a paragraph and it sounds like a competent update from a competent person. Nobody in that meeting can act on a single clause of it, and nobody will say so, because there is no error to point at.

LiveWhy this is worse than an obvious error4 min▶

An obvious error has one great virtue: it gets caught. A wrong number, a broken sentence, a claim your reader knows to be false - all of these trip a reviewer, get flagged, and get fixed. The cost is embarrassment, which is cheap and fast.

Plausible vagueness has the opposite profile. It survives review, because review is a search for things that are wrong and vagueness is not wrong. Then it does three quiet kinds of damage:

  • It transfers work to the reader. Someone has to go and find out what "considerably longer" means, and that someone is now more senior than you.
  • It launders the fact that you did not check. A blank space would have told the truth: this is unknown. "A fair amount" implies you looked.
  • It costs credibility slowly. Nobody remembers a single vague update. Everybody notices, over a year, whose writing they have to follow up on.

This is also why "is this AI writing?" is the wrong question, which a2 argues at manager altitude. The question that helps is "which claims in here could I check?" - and that question works identically on a draft you wrote by hand at midnight.

LiveThe lab proof: a brief without facts is half a brief3 min▶

You do not have to take this on faith - the ladder measures it. The unbriefed draft scores 48. Turn the brief lever on, with no facts supplied, and you get 52. Four points for the single practice everyone tells you is the whole game.

The brief did work: the point moved to the front, and there is now an ask at the bottom. But look at the specifics row. It barely moves, because there was nothing to be specific with, so the model filled the same holes with the same probable filler - just in a better order. A well-organised piece of vagueness is still vagueness, and it is arguably more dangerous, because the structure signals rigour.

Then watch what facts do. Add the register and the shape, and the specifics lever takes you from 70 to 83 - thirteen points from one slot, and the largest jump of any rung except voice and cutting. That is the whole argument for slot 4 in one number.

Part 3 · the habit

When the draft is wrong, change the brief 8 min live

Most people iterate on the draft. They read the output, dislike it, and reply "can you make it punchier" - and then again, and again, until they have spent fifteen minutes negotiating with a text box and produced something they would not have written themselves. The move that works is unglamorous: go back up a level and fix the input. A wrong draft is almost always a symptom, and the diagnosis is usually one empty slot.

LiveThree rounds on the Cadence update5 min▶

Round 1 - the vague request. What most people send, and it produces the 48-point draft: fluent, 223 words, opens with a paragraph about how the team has been working hard, closes by inviting questions.

Round 1 · no slots filled

"Write an update on the education proposal for the exec team. It's slipped a bit and there's some new customer research. Keep it professional."

Round 2 - add reader, decision and ask. The diagnosis after round 1 is not "the prose is flabby", it is "slots 1 to 3 are empty". Filling them gets you a piece that opens with the fork and ends with a request. It also gets you 52 points, and no further, because slot 4 is still empty.

Round 2 · reader, decision, ask

"Reader: our CFO, who approved the education plan in March and has not seen the new customer research. Decision: ship on the original date with the integration assumption untested, or slip four weeks while engineering settles it. Ask: reply by Thursday with ship or slip. Open with the decision, not with background. Under 150 words."

Round 3 - supply the facts. Twenty minutes of finding out, then five numbers pasted in. This is the round that changes the piece from an organised opinion into something a CFO can decide on.

Round 3 · the same brief plus slot 4

"Same brief. Use only these facts and do not add any others: the proposal has slipped two weeks; the decision is needed Thursday; academic procurement runs 6 to 12 months, so a four-week slip can cost a full cycle; 31 institutions are in the current pipeline; 48 customer samples moved the buyer from IT to academic budgets. If you need a fact I have not given you, write [MISSING] instead of estimating."

That last instruction is the cheapest safety net in this whole course. A model will honour it, and every [MISSING] in the output is a question you were about to answer with filler.

LiveThe follow-up that works, and the one that does not3 min▶

The difference between these two messages is about ten minutes of your life and roughly thirty points of clarity.

Iterating on the draft

"This is close but can you make it better? Punchier, more confident, and maybe tighten the middle section a bit."

Iterating on the brief

"The draft is vague because I did not give you the numbers. Here are the three you were missing: 31 institutions in pipeline, 6 to 12 month procurement cycle, 48 customer samples. Rewrite using these. Keep my structure and my sentence order, replace every vague quantity with the real figure, and delete any sentence that now repeats one of them."

Note what the good version is actually doing. It names the diagnosis, supplies the missing input, and constrains the rewrite so the model cannot wander off and re-flatten the voice while it is in there. "Make it better" gives it none of that, so it does the only thing it can do with no new information: it adds words. That is the mechanism behind the anti-lever - from a finished 98, "sound more professional" costs you 36 points and adds 42 words, because with nothing new to say, more professional can only mean more hedged.

Build-along

Prove it in the lab ★ 13 min · everyone runs it

This is the session's argument, computed. Start from nothing, turn on Brief by itself, and watch how little it buys: 48 to 52. Then build up to a real brief with facts in it and watch the specifics row climb to 83. The point of doing it in this order is to feel the difference between organising your vagueness and removing it.

All levers off: 48/100, 223 words. Find the specifics row and note it is at the floor. Read the draft and notice how reasonable it sounds anyway.

Turn on Brief alone: 52. Read the new opening - the point has moved up and there is an ask at the bottom. Now read the middle. "A number of institutions" is still there, because you gave it slots 1 to 3 and not slot 4.

Add Voice sample (68) and Structure (70). Two rungs, no new information - the register and the shape improved, and the piece still cannot be acted on.

Now add Specifics: 83. Watch the specifics row climb as 31 institutions, 48 samples, two weeks and Thursday arrive. Thirteen points from one slot.

Finish with Cut for 98 at 102 words, then press Sound more professional and watch the numbers go back out again - 62, and 42 words heavier.

What is written and what is measured The drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage for each missing practice. Every number in the scorecard is computed from the text above it: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density with a whitelist so ordinary words like "evidence" and "institutions" are not punished, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic - a be-verb followed by a participle - so read its count as a flag, not a census.
★ The line to remember from this session Brief without facts is worth 4 points. Facts on top of a brief are worth 13. The slot that takes real time is the slot that pays, and there is no prompt that substitutes for going and finding out.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Write a five-slot brief for one real piece of writing you owe someone. Time yourself. If it takes longer than three minutes, the slot that is slowing you down is the one telling you something.
  • Run the five tests on that brief. Be strict about slot 2: if your decision sentence contains the word "aware", find the real decision or drop the piece.
  • Take a draft a model wrote for you recently and mark every unfalsifiable quantity - a fair amount, several, considerably, in the near term. Count them. That count is the number of facts you did not supply.
  • Try the [MISSING] instruction on your next request: "if you need a fact I have not given you, write [MISSING] rather than estimating." Bring the list of [MISSING]s to b3 - it is usually the most honest document of the week.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which of these actually fills the reader slot?

The slot wants a person plus one thing they already believe that you are about to change. B gives you both, which tells you what to leave out and which fact leads. A mixes in register, and C is the awareness trap wearing a job title.

2 · You do not supply the numbers. What does the model do with the gap?

A gap is not a fluent continuation, so it produces the most probable phrase that fits an update. This is worse than an obvious error, because there is nothing for a reviewer to point at - it passes review and quietly hands the checking work to your reader.

3 · The draft came back vague. What is the move?

A wrong draft is a symptom, and usually of one empty slot. Nudging the words gives the model no new information, so it does the only thing it can - it adds. That is the same mechanism as the anti-lever, which costs 36 points and 42 extra words from a finished draft.
