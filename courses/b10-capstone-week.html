Capstone: one real week

Nine sessions of Cadence, and now the case is you. The capstone is not a fictional exercise and there is no model answer: you take one real week of your own writing, score it before, run the loop, score it after, and keep the pair. The pass condition is not a number - it is that you can say what changed and why, and that the word count came down while the scores went up. Then you write the thing you actually leave with, which is a one-page personal standard, and we talk honestly about which of these habits survive a busy month and which quietly die.

Part 0

What the track built

Every session left you an artifact rather than a feeling, which was deliberate: advice evaporates and artifacts sit in a folder. Before we start the capstone, collect them, because the week you are about to run uses all eight.

  • b2 - the five-slot brief. Reader, decision, ask, facts, register. Thirty seconds of writing that removes most of the drafting problem.
  • b3 - the voice doc and the banned list. Three paragraphs of your own writing plus the words you never use, so a model has something to match instead of a default corporate register.
  • b4 - the email patterns. Subject lines that carry the ask, the one-screen rule, and an ask nobody can misread.
  • b5 - the three format skeletons. Status update, decision memo, report. Written once, reused for years.
  • b6 - the sixty-second pass. Ask, specifics, hedges, passives, rhythm, abstraction, grade. The first two send a draft back; the rest you fix yourself.
  • b7 - the 40% drill and the constrained editor prompt. Cut to 60%, delete and rejoin only, no new words, keep every number.
  • b8 - the skip rule and the hard-genre patterns. The writing you do not hand over, plus how to structure bad news, pushback and a real apology.
  • b9 - the outline that holds. Argument sentence, section jobs, section briefs, the not-about list.

Part 1 · the assignment

One real week, scored twice 8 min live

Aim for five pieces you were already going to write: two emails, one update or memo, one difficult message, one longer piece. Score each one before you touch it, apply the loop, score it after, and write down the word count both times. The point of using real work is that a fictional exercise lets you perform the method. Your actual Tuesday does not.

LiveThe pass condition, stated precisely4 min▶

You have passed the capstone when both of these are true:

  • You can state what changed and why. Not "it got better" but "I cut the closing paragraph, deleted nine hedges, and added the two dates I had been vague about - and the ask moved from the last line to the first". If you cannot say what you did, you did not run a method, you had a good day.
  • The word count went down while the scores went up. That is the relationship the whole track is built on: 48 to 98 while 223 words became 102. Your numbers will be different and less dramatic. The direction is the thing.

Notice what is not the pass condition: a particular score. There is no threshold, and there is no leaderboard. A piece that went from 51 to 73 with 40% fewer words is a complete success, and the score-your-own box has no way of knowing whether you were right, which remains the only thing that actually matters.

LiveWhy chasing 98 on every piece is the wrong goal4 min▶

The lab's 98 is a demonstration that a briefed, voiced, structured, specific, cut draft measures well. It is not a target for your Tuesday, for three reasons:

  • Some pieces legitimately break the measures. A difficult message hedges on purpose, because you genuinely do not know how someone will react and pretending otherwise is dishonest. An apology should be short on specifics about blame. A condolence note scores terribly and should.
  • Optimising a number produces its own dead prose. Push any single measure hard enough and you get writing shaped by the measure rather than the reader: staccato sentences everywhere, numbers wedged into every clause.
  • The cost is real. Taking a routine email from 71 to 94 costs fifteen minutes that the reader will not notice. Spend those fifteen minutes on the memo that decides something.

The honest use of the score is triage: find the two pieces a week that are worth the pass, and let the rest go at 70.

Self-studyRunning the week without it eating the week3 min read▶

The capstone should cost about ninety minutes in total, spread across five days. It goes wrong in exactly one way: people schedule "capstone time" and then write new things for it. Do not write anything for this. Pick five pieces you already owe someone.

A workable shape: score before you start drafting, which takes twenty seconds per piece. Keep a single note file with five rows in it. Write the after-score the moment you send, not later, because the honest comparison depends on scoring the version that actually went out rather than the version you wish you had sent. And keep one full before-and-after pair - both texts, not just the numbers - because that pair is the only thing you will be able to show somebody, and it is worth more than the whole spreadsheet.

Part 2 · what you leave with

Your personal standard, five decisions 7 min live

The capstone is the exercise. The standard is the thing that outlives the course. It is one page, it has five decisions on it, and every one of them is a decision rather than an aspiration - which means it can be written down now, tonight, without doing any more work. If it takes longer than fifteen minutes, you are writing a manifesto instead.

Live1 · Your three most-used hedges2 min▶

Not the general list. Yours, the three you actually reach for, taken from the score-your-own box rather than from memory, because memory is flattering. Mine drift over time and I re-check them once a year.

Write them at the top of the page. The value is not that you stop using them, it is that you see them while typing, which turns a habit into a choice. Most people find that two of the three are load-bearing about a quarter of the time and pure padding the rest, and that distinction is exactly what the b6 read-aloud test settles: if you would not have said it in the room, take it out.

Live2 · The two formats you will template2 min▶

Two, not six. Look at your sent folder and find the two shapes you write most - for most people it is a status update and a request for a decision. Write the skeleton once, from b5, and keep it where you can paste it.

Templating is unglamorous and it is where the hours actually come back. A format you have decided in advance removes the blank-page problem, removes the structure lever from the drafting entirely, and makes it obvious when a piece is missing a part. Six templates die of neglect. Two get used every week and quietly become the way you write.

Live3 · Your skip rule2 min▶

Written in one line, from b1 and tested in b8: the kinds of writing you will not hand to a model. A common version reads: anything under four sentences, anything where the relationship is the content, and anything I cannot check.

Say it out loud to yourself once. The people who get the most out of AI writing are the ones with a clear rule for when not to use it, and the rule has to be specific enough to apply at 5pm on a bad day. "I use judgement" is not a rule. "Not condolences, not hard feedback, not anything three lines long" is.

Live4 · The one measure you will watch3 min▶

Pick one of the seven and watch only that one for the next few months. I recommend specifics, and the reason is not that it is the most important measure aesthetically. It is that it is the only one whose fix is not a writing fix.

Hedges, passives, rhythm and abstraction are all repairable at the keyboard in ten minutes. "Only two of nine sentences carry a number" cannot be edited away, because the missing number is not in the document and never was. Fixing it means going and finding something out - asking the question you had been rounding off, checking the date, reading the actual figure. That is why it changes behaviour fastest: it reaches back past the writing into the work the writing was supposed to report.

The side effect is the useful part. Once you know a specifics count is coming, you gather differently before you draft.

Live5 · The friction fix3 min▶

Decide where your voice doc lives, and make pasting it a four-second action. A pinned note, a text snippet with a keyboard shortcut, a file you keep open. It does not matter which, and it matters enormously that it is fast.

This is the least interesting item on the page and the one that determines whether any of the rest happens. The voice sample is the single biggest jump on the ladder at +16, and it is worth nothing at all if using it means opening three folders. Every practice in this track that survives a busy month survives because the friction is under about ten seconds. Anything above that is a practice you will do in week one, skip in week three, and feel guilty about in week five.

While you are there, do the same for your two templates and your banned-word list. One note, four seconds, done.

Part 3 · after the course

What survives a busy month 6 min live

I would rather be honest with you than encouraging. Some of what this track taught you will still be running in six months and some of it will be gone by the end of the month, and the pattern is predictable enough that you can plan around it. The practices that survive are cheap, run at the moment of sending, and pay out immediately. The ones that die are the ones that ask you to do extra work on something you already feel fine about.

LiveThe three that survive4 min▶
  • The sixty-second pass before sending anything that matters. It survives because it is attached to an action you already take, it costs a minute, and it catches a real embarrassment about once a fortnight. Attach a habit to sending, not to planning.
  • The 40% drill on anything over a page. It survives because the payoff is immediate and slightly shocking every time. Eight minutes, and the piece gets better in a way that is faintly insulting. Nobody who has done it three times stops.
  • The read-aloud test for hedges. It survives because it needs no tool, no file and no setup. If you would not have said "somewhat" in the room, delete it. This is the one that will still be running in five years, and it is the one I would keep if I could only keep one.

The common thread: zero setup, immediate payout, triggered by something you were doing anyway.

LiveThe three that quietly die4 min▶
  • Elaborate briefs for three-line emails. Correctly so. Writing a five-slot brief for "can we move Thursday to 2pm" is method theatre, and your instinct to skip it is right. The brief earns its keep above about half a page.
  • Scoring things you already like. This one dies for a bad reason and a good one. The good reason is that a piece you are happy with rarely needs a number. The bad reason is that we all quietly stop measuring the work we are proud of, which is exactly where a flat rhythm and a missing ask hide. Score one piece a month that you like.
  • Any practice with more than ten seconds of friction. Voice docs in buried folders, spreadsheets of scores, templates in a wiki nobody opens. Not a discipline failure. A design failure, and fixable by moving the file.

Plan for this rather than resolving harder. Keep the three that survive, fix the friction on the voice doc, and let the rest go without guilt.

Self-studyWhat the measures were actually for3 min read▶

Here is the honest closing point, and it undercuts the tool this whole track is built around: the measures are scaffolding. Seven rows and a total are not a theory of good writing, and they never were. They exist because "this feels a bit waffly" cannot be acted on, and because you needed something external while your own eye was being trained.

The goal is that in six months you do not need them. You read your own draft and see the eleven hedges without counting, notice the flat rhythm without measuring it, feel the absence of a number in a paragraph the way you feel a missing stair. At that point the box has done its job and you can stop opening it. Keep it around for the pieces that matter and for the moment you suspect your own judgement, which is a good instinct and worth honouring.

What is not scaffolding: deciding what you actually want to happen, checking that what you wrote is true, and being the person whose name is on it. No measurement touches any of those, and no tool will.

The capstone gate

Your real text, and the scoreboard one last time ★ 22 min · everyone runs it

Two boxes. The first is the tool you keep: paste something real, score it, cut it in place, score it again. The second is the ladder that has run under all ten sessions - 48 → 52 → 68 → 70 → 83 → 98, with 223 words falling to 102. Look at it once more as a shape rather than a set of numbers. Voice +16 and cut +15 are half the climb, neither is a prompting trick, and the score rose as the words fell every single rung except the specifics one, which is the only rung that buys words back.

Paste the first piece of your real week into the box below, before you have touched it. Record the total, the hedge count, the specifics count and the word count. Four numbers, twenty seconds.

Now run the loop on it: check the ask is in the first two lines, add the facts you were rounding off, delete the closing ritual paragraph, and cut the hedges you would not say out loud.

Score it again in the same box. Write down the same four numbers, then say out loud what changed and why. That sentence is the capstone, not the number.

Compare the direction of your two rows against the ladder in the second box. Yours will be smaller and it should point the same way: score up, words down.

Finally, press the anti-lever on the ladder one last time. 62, minus 36 points. That is the request you will now recognise for the rest of your career.

What is written and what is measured The ladder's drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage for each missing practice. Every number on both boxes is computed from the text: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic - a be-verb plus a participle - so read its count as a flag rather than a census. Nothing you paste leaves your browser, and no score can tell whether what you wrote is true.
Real world

The step nobody does is the last one. In the Nielsen Norman Group diary study "Information Foraging with Generative AI" (24 September 2023) - two weeks, 18 participants, 425 conversations - only 22.43% of conversations were followed by any verification at all. That is the strongest available number on people not checking the output, and it is the reason step 5 of the loop is called own rather than review. Every measure in this track improves how a draft reads. None of them can tell you that the date is wrong, that the figure came from last quarter, or that the person you named left in March. Your signature is on the piece either way, which is the whole reason a scoring tool is scaffolding and your judgement is the standard.

After the course

Four things, then you are done ◐ 90 min across one week

  • Run the week. Five real pieces, scored before and after, word counts both times. Do not write anything new for it. Ninety minutes total, spread across five days, and the pass condition is that you can say what changed and why.
  • Keep one before-and-after pair as proof. Both full texts, not just the numbers. It is the only artifact from this track that persuades anybody else, including the version of you who is tempted to skip the pass in November.
  • Teach the sixty-second pass to one colleague. Seven checks, in order, on a document one of you actually has to approve. Teaching it is how you find out which parts you understood, and it is also how a personal habit turns into a team norm without anybody writing a policy.
  • Choose the next thing to learn. If you now have to set a standard for other people's writing rather than your own, go to the leader track: a3 for capturing a house voice from samples rather than adjectives, and a5 for the one-page team writing standard. If you would rather go deeper on your own craft, the neighbours own the rest - AI + Content for marketing and publishing, Tech Writing for documentation, Prompt Engineering for prompting as a discipline.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is the capstone's pass condition?

There is no threshold and no leaderboard. A piece that went from 51 to 73 with 40% fewer words is a complete success. What matters is that you ran a method you can describe - "I cut the closing paragraph, deleted nine hedges, added two dates, moved the ask to the first line" - rather than simply having a good day at the keyboard.

2 · Which single measure is worth watching for the next few months, and why that one?

Hedges, passives, rhythm and abstraction are all repairable at the keyboard in ten minutes. A missing number cannot be edited in, because it is not in the document and never was. Fixing specifics means going and finding something out, which is why it changes behaviour fastest and why you start gathering differently before you draft.

3 · What are the seven measures ultimately for?

They exist because "this feels a bit waffly" cannot be acted on. Once you see the hedges without counting and feel a missing number the way you feel a missing stair, the box has done its job. What never becomes automatic, and what no measurement touches, is deciding what you want to happen, checking that what you wrote is true, and being the person whose name is on it.
