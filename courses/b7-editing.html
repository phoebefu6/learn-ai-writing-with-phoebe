Editing: cut, restructure, kill the hedges

This is the session most people need most, so it is the most practical page in the track: a procedure, not an idea. You take a draft, remove 40% of the words, and remove no information. There is an order that works, and following it means you almost never have to rewrite. In the lab, cut alone is worth +15 points - the biggest single jump on the ladder alongside voice, and the one nobody delegates well, because a model was trained to be helpful and adding feels helpful. Tonight you do the drill on a real draft, then on your own.

Part 0

Where this sits in the track

b6 gave you the diagnosis: seven checks you can run by eye in a minute, and the two cuts that do most of the work. This session is the treatment, and it is deliberately mechanical. Editing has a reputation as the taste-heavy part of writing, which is only true of the last 10%. The first 90% is a set of moves you can learn in one evening and run for the rest of your career, in a fixed order, without any judgement about what the piece should sound like. Get through that 90% first and you will find you have far more room left for the judgement.

Part 1 · the procedure

The 40% drill, in the order that works 9 min live

Take any draft. Remove 40% of the words. Remove no information. Most people hear that and reach for the sentences they like least, which is why the drill fails for them - they start rewriting on pass one and spend twenty minutes polishing prose that was always going to be deleted. The order below exists to stop that. Every pass is a deletion except the last one, and by the time you reach the last one there is usually nothing left to rewrite. In the lab this whole procedure is one lever, and that lever is worth +15 points: the same size as the voice sample, from work that requires no writing skill at all.

LivePass by pass, with the actual moves5 min▶

Run these in order on a real draft. Do not skip ahead, and do not tidy as you go:

  • 1 - the closing ritual paragraph. The one that thanks the reader for their time, restates the opening and invites questions. Delete the whole block, not parts of it. It is the single largest chunk you will remove and it takes four seconds.
  • 2 - the hedges you would not say out loud. Read the draft aloud. Anywhere you would not have said "somewhat", "arguably", "I think", "a number of" in the room, take it out. The ones that survive are your genuine uncertainty, and those get promoted into a plain sentence: "we do not know whether X, and here is what would settle it."
  • 3 - nominalisations back into verbs. "The implementation of the change" becomes "we changed". "There was a delay in the delivery" becomes "we delivered late". Watch what comes back with the verb: a person. That is the real win, not the word count.
  • 4 - collapse the restatements. Look for any two sentences saying the same thing at different volumes, which generated prose produces constantly. Keep the quieter one. It is almost always the more specific of the two.
  • 5 - adverbs doing emotional work. "Significantly", "extremely", "unfortunately", "really" are volume knobs. Delete them. Adverbs carrying fact - "twice weekly", "internally", "manually" - stay, because deleting those loses information.
  • 6 - only now, rewrite. Read what is left. Usually two or three sentences need joining and one needs a new opening clause. If you find yourself rewriting more than that, you do not have an editing job - see Part 2.

Eight minutes, start to finish, on a page of prose. The first time you do it the piece gets better in a way that is slightly insulting.

LiveWhy the order matters more than the moves3 min▶

Every move on that list is obvious. The order is the part that is worth teaching, for three reasons:

  • Deleting whole blocks first makes the later passes smaller. The closing paragraph is usually where a third of the hedges live. Kill it on pass one and pass two has less to do.
  • Deletion is reversible in a way rewriting is not. You can paste a deleted sentence back. You cannot un-rewrite a paragraph once you have lost the version you started with, and rewritten prose tends to drift towards a flatter register - yours or a model's.
  • Rewriting early hides the real problem. If a paragraph will not come out right after two attempts, the trouble is nearly always structural, and polishing it is a way of avoiding that. Deleting first exposes the structure fast, because a piece with the padding gone has nowhere to hide its shape.

The one-sentence version: delete before you decide, decide before you write.

Self-studyThe hedges that earn their place3 min read▶

Pass two is the one people overrun. Deleting every hedge produces a draft that sounds more certain than you are, which is a worse failure than waffling because it is dishonest rather than merely slow. Three that stay:

  • A real confidence level. "The 14 March date is firm; the September rollout is a guess" is not hedging, it is information. Keep it and make it sharper.
  • A stated limit on what you checked. "This is from two schools, not all six" belongs in the draft. Deleting it makes the piece score better and makes you less trustworthy.
  • Genuine deference where the decision is not yours. "If you would rather hold this until Q4, say so and I will replan" is a real sentence with a real ask in it.

The test is not whether a word appears on a hedge list. It is whether the hedge is carrying information about your confidence, or covering the fact that you did not want to commit. The first is honesty. The second is what the drill is for.

Part 2 · the bigger move

Restructuring without rewriting from zero 7 min live

Sometimes the drill finishes and the piece is shorter and still wrong. That is a structure problem, and the temptation is to start again from a blank page - which throws away every fact you already gathered and usually produces a draft with the same shape as the first one. You almost never need to start again. You need to move three or four blocks and promote one sentence. The moves below are cheap, and the last card is the test that tells you which kind of job you are on.

LiveMove the last paragraph to the top2 min▶

This is right far more often than it has any business being. Writers think their way to the point and then stop, so the conclusion arrives after the reasoning that produced it. The reader wants the opposite order.

Try it mechanically: cut the final paragraph, paste it at the top, and read the piece again. Three things usually happen at once. The old opening turns out to be a windup you can delete entirely. The middle sections stop needing their linking sentences, because the reader already knows where this is going. And the ask becomes findable in the first four seconds, which is the only reading window most of your documents actually get.

If moving it makes the piece worse, that is useful too - it means the ending was a summary rather than a conclusion, and summaries do belong at the end.

LiveFind the sentence that is actually your point, and promote it2 min▶

In most drafts there is one sentence doing the real work, and it is buried in the third paragraph with a hedge in front of it. It often starts with "so really the question is", or "what this means is", or "to be honest".

Find it. Delete the run-up phrase. Put it first. Then read every other paragraph and ask what it is doing for that sentence - supporting it, qualifying it, or nothing. The paragraphs doing nothing are the ones you have been unable to cut because they were "background", and once the point is visible at the top their pointlessness is obvious.

This move is why the drill puts rewriting last. If you had rewritten paragraph three, you would have smoothed the very sentence you were looking for into something more polite and less findable.

LiveCut it in half by choosing one audience2 min▶

A document that will not come under control is often two documents. At Cadence this week, the education note is trying to be a decision memo for the exec team and a delivery briefing for the engineers at the same time, so every section carries both a "what this means for the number" paragraph and a "how we would build it" paragraph. Neither reader needs the other half, and both of them stop reading.

Decide which one it is for. Delete the other reader's half entirely rather than trimming it - trimming leaves stubs that read like unfinished thoughts. If the second audience genuinely needs something, they get their own short document, which will take you eight minutes because you already wrote it.

Two clean documents beat one that serves nobody, and the total word count usually still falls.

Self-studyThe test: can you write the one-line summary?3 min read▶

Before you spend twenty minutes editing sentences, try to write one line that says what the piece is and what you want. Not a title. A sentence with a subject, a verb and an ask: "the education proposal slips to 14 March because two of three schools moved their budget cycle, and I need a go or no-go on the reduced scope by Thursday."

  • If the line comes out easily, you have an editing job. Run the drill, move the point to the top, ship it.
  • If the line needs two clauses joined by "and also", you have two documents. Use the audience split.
  • If you cannot write the line at all, no sentence-level work will save the piece. Nobody decided what it was for, and that is a thinking problem wearing a writing problem's clothes. Go back to the brief from b2 - reader, decision, ask, facts, register - and fill in the slot that is empty. That is usually the ask.

This is the same failure as check 1 in b6's sixty-second pass, seen from the writer's side rather than the reviewer's. Both of them exist to stop you editing a document that should not exist yet.

Part 3 · the tool, constrained

Editing with a model, under constraint 6 min live

A model is a poor editor by default and a genuinely useful one under constraint, and it is worth understanding exactly why rather than treating it as a quirk. Two biases do all the damage. It adds when asked to improve, because it was trained to be helpful and adding feels helpful - ask for a better paragraph and you get a longer one. It agrees when asked whether something is clear, because agreement is the helpful-sounding answer, so "is this clear?" returns "yes, this is clear and well structured" on almost anything. Neither is a bug you can prompt away. Both are things you design around.

LiveBias one: it adds. So make the ceiling the instruction3 min▶

"Shorter" is not a constraint. A number, plus a ban on new material, is:

Invites the failure

"Can you improve this and make it tighter and more polished?"

Constrains it into usefulness

"Cut this to 60% of its current length. Delete and rejoin only - do not add any new words, ideas or transitions. Keep every number, name and date. Keep my sentence order. Then list separately: every hedge you removed, and any sentence where you think I am hiding an uncertainty rather than stating it."

Each clause is doing a specific job. "60%" is a hard ceiling it can be held to. "Delete and rejoin only" blocks the smoothing transitions it would otherwise invent. "Keep every number, name and date" stops the specifics being generalised away, which is the most expensive thing an editing pass can do to you. "Keep my sentence order" keeps the diff readable. And the two lists at the end are the actually valuable output: the hedge list is a record of your own habits, and the hiding-an-uncertainty list is the closest a model gets to useful criticism.

LiveBias two: it agrees. So never ask it whether the writing is good3 min▶

Change the question from a verdict to an argument, and give it a side to argue:

Guaranteed to be reassuring and useless

"Is this clear? Does the ask come across?"

Forces something you can act on

"Argue that my reader will misunderstand the ask. Quote the exact sentence they will misread, say what they will think I am asking for, and name what they will do instead of what I want."

The pattern generalises. Never ask "is this good", ask it to make the case against. "List every fact in here a reader could challenge and what they would ask for." "Read this as the person whose project I am delaying." You are not getting judgement out of it, you are getting coverage - a list of the objections you would otherwise discover in the meeting.

Self-studyIt flattens your voice while tidying, so diff rather than accept3 min read▶

Even a well-constrained cut comes back slightly less like you. Editing touches exactly the features that make prose recognisable: the odd sentence length, the plain word where a formal one was available, the joke you nearly took out yourself. Accept three rounds of that wholesale and you have prose that could have come from anyone, which is a slow loss you will not notice happening.

  • Read the diff, not the output. Paste both versions side by side and go line by line. Take the deletions you agree with and refuse the substitutions.
  • Give it your voice sample anyway. This is why b3 matters at the editing stage and not just the drafting stage: the same three paragraphs of your own writing, handed over with "match this register", keep a cut from drifting corporate.
  • Watch for the reinstated hedge. A model that is asked to cut will sometimes hedge what remains, because a shorter sentence sounds blunter to it. That is the anti-lever in miniature: from a finished draft, "sound more professional" costs 36 points and adds 42 words, and every substitution it makes is a hedge, a passive or an abstract noun.
  • It cannot check your facts. Editing and verifying are different jobs. The numbers are yours, always, and a model will happily preserve a wrong one beautifully.
Build-along

Turn Cut on, then do it to your own draft ★ 20 min · everyone runs it

The first box starts at 83, with brief, voice, structure and specifics already in place. This is a genuinely decent draft: briefed, in a human register, with real numbers in it. Everything left to gain is deletion. Turn Cut on and it goes to 98 while the word count falls from 156 to 102 - that is the whole of tonight's session, computed in front of you.

Read the 83-point draft first and find the closing paragraph, the two hedges and the restatement by eye. Predict which rows will move before you click anything.

Turn Cut on. Score 83 to 98, words 156 to 102. Check your predictions: the hedge row and the rhythm row move most, because deleting a ritual paragraph and two hedges also breaks up the steady sentence length.

Note what does not move. Specifics stay high, because the drill removed no information - that is the whole claim of the 40% drill, visible as a row that holds steady while the total climbs 15 points.

Now the second box. Paste something you actually sent this week and run the six passes in the box, in order, watching the score after each pass. Closing paragraph first. Do not rewrite until pass six.

Record two numbers: the score before, and the word count before and after. If you did not lose at least 30% of the words, you rewrote too early - undo and start again at pass one.

What is written and what is measured The drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage per missing practice. Every number in the scorecard is computed from the text above it: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic - a be-verb plus a participle - so read its count as a flag rather than a census. The score also cannot see whether you are right: a well-cut, specific paragraph of nonsense scores 98.
Real world

The 40% drill is the only writing exercise I have seen change someone's writing permanently in one sitting. Not because deleting is clever, but because of what it teaches by accident: after you have thrown away 40% of a page you wrote, you start noticing the padding as you type it. People come back from this drill writing shorter first drafts, which is the actual prize. The lab number says +15 points. The real return is that the draft you write next week starts at 60% of the length it used to.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 35-45 min total

  • Run the full six-pass drill on three real drafts, in order, timing each one. Keep both versions of all three. By the third you should be under six minutes, and you should be able to feel which pass your own writing loses most words to.
  • Take a document you have already sent and try the two structural moves on it: last paragraph to the top, then promote the buried point. Do not touch a single sentence otherwise. Note whether the piece needed structure or editing, and whether you guessed right before you started.
  • Use the constrained cut prompt on a paragraph you like, then read the diff line by line instead of the output. Mark every place it flattened your voice, and every hedge it quietly put back.
  • Write the one-line summary for the piece of writing you are currently avoiding. If you cannot, you have found out something more useful than any edit would have told you - and b8 is about the kind of writing where that happens most.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the 40% drill, why does rewriting come last rather than first?

Five of the six passes are deletions, and they run first so that the sixth has almost nothing left to do. You can paste a deleted sentence back; you cannot un-rewrite a paragraph once the original is gone. Rewriting early also hides structural trouble, because a smoothed paragraph stops announcing that it is in the wrong place.

2 · You hand a model a paragraph and ask it to improve this. What comes back, and why?

Unconstrained improvement requests reliably grow the text, which is exactly backwards from what editing is. The fix is not a politer prompt but a hard ceiling plus a ban on new material: "cut to 60%, delete and rejoin only, no new words, ideas or transitions, keep every number, name and date". Then ask it to list what it removed so you can restore anything load-bearing.

3 · What tells you the piece needs restructuring rather than editing?

If the one-line summary will not come, no sentence-level work will save the piece - nobody decided what it was for. If the line needs two clauses joined by "and also", you have two documents and should split by audience. Only when the line comes out easily is the drill the right tool, and that is the same failure as check 1 of b6's pass, seen from the writer's side.
