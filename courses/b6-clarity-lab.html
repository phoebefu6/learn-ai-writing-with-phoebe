The clarity lab: a minute, not a mood

"This feels a bit waffly" cannot be acted on. "Eleven hedges, no numbers in six sentences, and every sentence the same length" can be fixed before your coffee goes cold. This session turns the seven measures into a sixty-second pass you can run by eye on anything - your draft, a model's draft, or a document someone just sent you and asked you to approve. Then we do the hard half: what to actually delete, and why a model is the worst editor you will ever work with unless you constrain it savagely.

Part 1 · the standard

Seven checks, in this order 8 min live

Order matters, because the first two checks often make the rest unnecessary. Start with the ask - if you cannot find what the reader is meant to do, stop reading and go back to the writer. Then specifics, because a piece with no numbers or names is usually not a draft problem but a thinking problem. Only then work through the surface habits: hedges, passives, rhythm, abstraction, tells. Grade level last, as a sanity check.

LiveThe sentence that sends a draft back4 min▶

The hardest part of this is not diagnosis, it is saying it to a colleague without sounding like you are marking homework. The form that works names the absence, not the person:

Vague and unactionable

"Thanks for this. I think it needs a bit more work - it feels a bit long and I am not quite sure what the takeaway is. Maybe tighten it up?"

Specific and fixable in twenty minutes

"Two things. I cannot find the ask - what do you want the exec team to decide, and by when? And only two of the nine sentences carry a number, so the risks read as feelings rather than facts. Fix those two and I suspect the length problem solves itself."

The last clause matters. Most length complaints are really specificity complaints: a piece that says nothing has to keep talking. Once the numbers arrive, the padding has nowhere left to hide.

LiveThe two cuts that do most of the work4 min▶

In the lab, "cut" is worth 15 points on its own - the single biggest jump on the ladder - and it is almost entirely two moves:

  • Delete the closing paragraph. The one that thanks the reader for their time, restates the opening, and invites questions. It is pure ritual. Anyone who has questions will ask them without being invited, and the reader's last impression should be your ask, not your politeness.
  • Delete the hedges you would not say out loud. Read the draft aloud. Anywhere you would not have said "somewhat", "arguably" or "I think" in the room, take it out. What is left is the uncertainty you actually have, and that deserves a plain sentence of its own: "we do not know whether X, and here is what would settle it."

The 40% drill: take any draft and remove 40% of the words without removing any information. It sounds impossible and takes about eight minutes. The first time you do it, the piece gets better in a way that is slightly insulting.

Self-studyUsing a model as an editor - and its two biases4 min read▶

Models are poor editors by default and useful editors under constraint, for a reason worth understanding: they were trained to be helpful, and adding feels helpful. Ask one to "improve" a paragraph and it will usually return a longer paragraph.

Invites the failure

"Can you improve this and make it more polished?"

Constrains it into usefulness

"Cut this to 60% of its current length. Do not add any new words, ideas or transitions - only delete and rejoin. Keep every number, name and date. Keep my sentence order. Then list separately: every hedge you removed, and any sentence where you think I am hiding an uncertainty rather than stating it."

  • Bias one: it adds. Always give a word budget as a hard ceiling, and forbid new content explicitly. "Shorter" is not a constraint; "60% and no new words" is.
  • Bias two: it agrees. Ask it whether a draft is clear and it will tell you it is clear. Ask it to argue that your reader will misunderstand the ask, and you get something useful.
  • It cannot check your facts. Editing and verifying are different jobs. The numbers are yours, always.
  • It flattens voice while tidying. Diff its output against yours rather than accepting wholesale, or three rounds of editing will leave you with prose that could have come from anyone. This is why b3's voice sample matters even at the editing stage.
Part 2 · the shape of the whole course

The score rises as the words fall 5 min live

The ladder is not just six increasing numbers. It is two lines moving in opposite directions - and once you have seen it, "can you make this longer" stops sounding like a reasonable request.

Self-studyReading a document you did not write3 min read▶

The same pass works when the writing arrives in your inbox for approval. Two adjustments:

  • Do not ask whether AI wrote it. You cannot reliably tell, the question puts people on the defensive, and it is the wrong question anyway - a human can write fluent emptiness perfectly well without help. Ask whether it says something checkable.
  • Ask for the brief, not the process. "Who is this for and what do you want them to do?" gets you a better second draft than any comment on the first one. If the answer is vague, the draft was always going to be.

Demo 1 of 2

Work backwards from good ★ 13 min · everyone runs it

Start at 98, with all five practices in place, and take them away one at a time. Working backwards teaches faster than climbing, because you feel what each habit was buying you rather than watching a number rise for reasons you have not yet internalised.

Read the 98-point draft out loud. 102 words, eight sentences, one ask. Note how much of its quality is subtraction rather than skill.

Turn Cut off. Watch the closing ritual paragraph reappear and the hedges creep back into three sentences. 15 points gone.

Turn Specifics off. Every number becomes "a fair amount", "considerably longer", "some early research". This is the most dangerous state a draft can be in, because it still reads fine.

Turn Voice sample off. The corporate register arrives and brings passives with it. Read the difference aloud - this is the rung people underestimate most.

Turn everything off, then press Sound more professional on top. This is the document that lands in inboxes every day.

What is written and what is measured The drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage per missing practice. Every number is computed from the text above it: hedge and tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic, so read its count as a flag rather than a census.

The useful part is not the score, it is the hedge list: seeing your own crutch words counted is a permanent change in how you write.

Paste an email you sent this week. Read the hedge row first, then the specifics row. Ignore the total for now.

Delete 40% of the words in the box, in place, without losing information. Watch the score while you do it.

Now paste a paragraph a model drafted for you and compare the shape of the failures. Yours will be hedges; its will be rhythm and abstraction.

Write down your three most-used hedges. That is your personal edit list for the rest of the course.

Real world

The measure that changes behaviour fastest is specifics, not hedges. Hedging is a habit and habits are stubborn. But "only two of nine sentences carry a number" is not a habit, it is a signal that you have not yet done the work the writing was supposed to report. Most of the time, fixing that means going and finding something out - and the draft then writes itself in half the words. That is why check 2 in the pass sends the piece back rather than into an edit: no amount of editing can add a fact that nobody looked up.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Run the sixty-second pass by eye on five things in your sent folder, then check yourself against the lab. Record where your eye was wrong - most people underestimate their own hedging and overestimate their specificity.
  • Do the 40% drill on one real draft. Time it. Keep both versions so you can show someone the difference.
  • Use the constrained editor prompt on a paragraph you like, then diff its output against yours and mark every place it flattened your voice.
  • Write the send-it-back sentence for a document you actually received recently, using the two-things form from Part 1. You do not have to send it - the point is being able to name the absence.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Checks 1 and 2 of the pass are treated differently from the rest. Why?

Hedges, passives and rhythm are all fixable by you in ten minutes. A missing ask means nobody decided what the writing was for, and missing numbers mean the underlying work is not done. Editing cannot supply either, which is why those two send the draft back.

2 · You ask a model to "improve" a paragraph. What is the most likely outcome?

Unconstrained editing requests reliably grow the text. Give a hard ceiling, forbid new content explicitly ("cut to 60%, delete and rejoin only, no new words or transitions"), and ask it to list what it removed so you can put back anything that was load-bearing.

3 · Across the ladder the score climbs 48 to 98. What happens to the word count?

Nothing was removed from the message, only the words that were not carrying it. The specifics rung does add words, and the cut rung removes far more than it costs. That inverse relationship is the argument of the whole course, which is why "can you make this longer" should always be met with a question.
