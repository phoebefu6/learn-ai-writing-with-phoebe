Updates, reports, decision memos

Almost everything you write at work has the same shape underneath it: the point, the three things behind it, the ask. Decide that shape before you draft and the rhythm varies on its own, the ask becomes findable, and the reading grade stops climbing for no reason. Skip it and the same content welds itself into one flat wall. Tonight: the three formats worth having skeletons for, the status update that should be deleted rather than written, and the block most memos are missing - the honest unknown, written so that not knowing costs something specific.

Part 1 · decide the shape before you draft

The point, the three things, the ask 8 min live

This shape covers the weekly update, the decision memo, the report summary, the project post-mortem and most of what you will ever be asked to write at work. The point in the first line, stated as a claim rather than a topic. The three things behind it, each one a fact you could be challenged on. The ask, with an owner and a date. Three is not magic; it is the number of supporting claims a busy reader will hold at once, and if you have seven you have a report with an appendix rather than a memo.

Deciding the shape first is what produces varied rhythm, which is the thing people notice and cannot name. Once you know that line one is a claim, line two starts a numbered fact, and the last block is an ask, the sentences take different lengths because they are doing different jobs. Skip the shape and every sentence has the same job - keep going - so they all come out the same length. That is the flat 18-to-22-word hum, and it is why the reading grade rises in an unstructured draft even though nothing in it got smarter. Nothing got harder to understand either. It just got harder to enter.

LiveWhy structure is worth two points and changes everything4 min▶

On the ladder, structure moves the total from 68 to 70. Two points. It is the smallest rung on the climb, and it is the one I would defend hardest, because the total is measuring surface habits and structure is not a surface habit.

Look at what it does move. Rhythm improves, because a claim, a numbered fact and an ask are naturally different lengths. Reading grade comes down, because the shape breaks the long welded sentences into shorter ones with fewer subordinate clauses. And the thing the score cannot see at all improves most: findability. A reader can locate your ask in two seconds instead of reading 160 words hoping one of them is a request.

So treat structure as the lever whose payoff is mostly invisible to measurement. This is a useful general lesson about the lab: a score is a way of catching habits, not a definition of quality. b6 makes that argument properly. For now, the practical version is that you should decide the shape even though the number barely moves.

LiveDeciding the shape takes ninety seconds3 min▶

Before drafting anything, write these four lines in a scratch file. Not prose, just answers:

  • The point. One sentence, a claim, no windup. "The education proposal slips two weeks and I recommend we hold the launch to October."
  • Thing one. "The evidence says institutions hold the budget, not students, so the whole pricing model changes."
  • Thing two. "Recording lectures needs documented consent, which adds a legal step we had not scoped."
  • Thing three. "Procurement at these institutions runs 6 to 12 months, from a study of 31 of them."
  • The ask. "Priya approves the revised scope by Thursday 5pm. Default if I do not hear: we hold."

That is the memo. Everything after it is expansion, and expansion is the part a model can genuinely help with, because the decisions are already made. If you cannot write those five lines, drafting will not rescue you - you do not yet have a memo, you have a topic.

One warning. If your three things are all the same thing said three ways, you have one thing and a length target. Say the one thing and stop.

Part 2 · three skeletons to copy

The update, the memo, the report 8 min live

Three formats, three skeletons. Copy them, fill them, and stop re-inventing the container every time. Notice that all three are the same shape with different emphasis: the update leans on what changed, the memo on the claims carrying a recommendation, the report on findings with the method moved out of the reader's way.

LiveThe status update: what changed, what it means, what I need4 min▶

Three headings, and the third one is why anyone reads it:

  • What changed since the last update. Facts with numbers and dates. Not activity - "we held three workshops" is activity; "the education proposal slipped two weeks" is a change.
  • What it means. Your interpretation, marked as yours. This is where you earn your salary: anyone can list events, and the person reading needs to know which of them matter.
  • What I need. An owner, an action, a date. If the answer is genuinely nothing, write "nothing needed this week" and mean it.

Then the hard rule. An update that exists to prove effort should be deleted rather than written. If nothing changed, nothing means anything new, and you need nothing, the honest update is one line: "no material change on education this week; next decision point is Thursday." Sending 300 words instead is not diligence. It costs every reader ninety seconds, teaches them that your updates are skippable, and means the week you do have something urgent it arrives in a channel they have learned to skim.

The test I use: delete the update and ask what breaks. If the answer is "nothing, but it looks bad", the update was theatre, and the fix is a conversation with whoever needs the theatre rather than more words.

LiveThe decision memo, block by block4 min▶

The document you write when you need someone else to commit. Five blocks, in this order, and the order is the argument:

  • The decision. Name it as a choice with sides. "Hold the education launch to October, or ship in July." A reader who does not know what is being decided cannot evaluate anything that follows.
  • The recommendation, with a confidence word. One of high, moderate or low, chosen deliberately. "Recommend holding. Confidence: moderate." Confidence words are cheap to write and expensive to fake, and using them consistently is one of the fastest ways to become someone whose memos get believed.
  • The two or three claims carrying it. Not everything you know. The claims that would change the decision if they turned out to be wrong. Each one with its source attached.
  • The honest unknown. Part 3 is entirely about this block, because it is the one most memos are missing.
  • The ask. Owner, date, and the default if the date passes. Same four parts as b4's email ask.

Cross-link worth knowing: this is exactly the memo the AI + Research course's capstone produces, and the Cadence education question is the same running case in both. That course is about doing the research and auditing the claims; this one is about the document you write when the research is done. If you have taken it, the claims block is where your source audit lands, and the confidence word is where your evidence grade goes.

LiveThe report: findings first, method in an appendix3 min▶

Reports go wrong in one predictable way: they are written in the order the work happened. Background, then approach, then what we did, then data, then finally, on page nine, what we found. That order is the writer's chronology, not the reader's need.

  • Findings first, as claims with numbers. Three to five of them, each one a sentence you would defend in a meeting.
  • So what, second. What each finding means for a decision someone has to make. A finding with no consequence is a fact you collected, not a finding.
  • Method in an appendix. How you sampled, what you excluded, where the data came from, what you would do differently. It must be there and it must not be first.
  • Limitations named, not buried. One short section, in plain words, before the appendix.

The principle underneath: never make a reader work through your process to reach your conclusion. Doing so reads as rigour and functions as a toll. The reader who wants your method will go and find it in the appendix, and will think better of you for having put it there. The reader who does not want it - which is most of them, most of the time - gets to the finding on line one and can start thinking.

Self-studyConfidence words, used consistently3 min read▶

A confidence word is only worth writing if it means the same thing every time you use it. A working definition you can adopt as-is:

  • High - I have direct evidence, from a source I can name, that I would defend in front of the person who owns the number.
  • Moderate - the evidence points this way but the sample, the source or the transfer to our situation is imperfect. I would not be shocked to be wrong.
  • Low - I have a view and thin support for it. I am telling you so you can decide whether to spend money finding out.

Two habits that make them work. Write the word before you write the paragraph, so the prose has to match the label rather than the other way round. And go back after the decision resolves to check whether your moderates were actually moderate. Calibration is a skill and almost nobody practises it, which means the person who does becomes noticeably more trusted within about six months.

Part 3 · the block most memos are missing

The unknown, written honestly 7 min live

Here is the Cadence situation in one sentence: whether the notes engine transfers to lecture audio is untested, and the only supporting evidence is a vendor-funded preprint with 48 samples. That assumption is load-bearing - the pricing depends on it - and it is the weakest thing in the memo. What you do with that sentence is the difference between a memo that gets believed and one that gets nodded at.

Three wrong moves, all of them common, all of them tempting. Softening it into "early evidence suggests strong transfer" turns 48 vendor-funded samples into a finding, which is a small lie that a reader will discover at exactly the worst moment. Padding it with three more agreeable sources so the paragraph looks well-supported is worse, because it adds volume in place of evidence and a careful reader will notice that none of the sources tested the thing you need tested. And deleting it is the most common of all: the assumption never appears, so the reader agrees to a plan whose central risk they were not shown. The right move is smaller than any of those.

LiveMark it, price it, name the test4 min▶

Three moves, one sentence each, and they always go in this order:

  • Mark it unknown. Use the word. "Untested." Not "an area for further exploration", which is a way of writing "untested" while hoping nobody notices.
  • Say what not knowing costs. This is the move that makes an unknown useful rather than decorative. If transfer does not hold, the pricing model built on it is wrong, so we should not price on it until we know.
  • Name the test that would settle it. A specific, bounded, cheap-enough test with a duration. This converts the unknown from a worry into a decision the reader can actually make: spend two weeks, or accept the risk knowingly.
Softened, padded, and quietly misleading

"Early evidence suggests the notes engine should transfer well to lecture audio, and the broader literature on speech recognition in educational settings is generally encouraging, so we are reasonably confident this will not be a significant blocker to the proposed pricing approach."

Marked, priced, and testable

"Whether the notes engine transfers to lecture audio is untested. The only evidence is a vendor-funded preprint with 48 samples, which is not enough to price on. A two-week benchmark on 20 real lecture recordings would settle it, and until then we should not price on transfer working."

Read the two aloud. The first is longer, warmer and says nothing a reader can act on. The second is shorter, admits the weakness, and hands the reader a decision. In practice the second one gets you the two weeks, because you asked for something specific in exchange for something specific.

LiveWhy the honest version reads as more competent3 min▶

The fear is that naming an unknown makes you look like you have not done the work. In practice it does the opposite, for a reason worth understanding: a reader who is any good already suspects there is a weak assumption in your memo. Their question is whether you know where it is.

If you name it, priced and testable, you have demonstrated that you audited your own argument. Everything else in the memo becomes more credible, because the reader now believes you would have flagged any other weakness too. If you hide it and they find it in the meeting, the cost is not that one claim - it is that every other claim now needs checking.

There is a version of this that goes wrong, though, and it is worth naming: hedging everything is not honesty. A memo where every sentence is qualified has not audited itself, it has abdicated. The discipline is to be plain about the two or three things you actually do not know, and unhedged about everything else. That is why hedge-counting and unknown-marking are not in tension: you delete the hedges you would not say out loud, and then write one clear sentence about the thing you genuinely cannot support.

Self-studyReading a memo for its missing block3 min read▶

When a memo arrives for your approval, the fastest useful question is not about the writing at all: where is the weakest assumption, and did they tell me?

  • Find the number the recommendation rests on, then find its source. If the source is a vendor, a preprint, a small sample or a different context, that is the unknown - whether or not the memo called it one.
  • Watch for the softening vocabulary. "Early evidence suggests", "generally encouraging", "broadly aligned", "should be manageable". These are almost always sitting on top of something the writer knows is thin.
  • Count the sources per claim. A claim with four agreeable citations and no test is usually padded. One source that actually tested the thing beats four that discussed it.
  • Ask for the test, not the certainty. "What would settle this, and what would it cost?" is a question that improves a memo in one round, and it teaches the writer the habit for next time.

Build-along

Structure on, structure off ★ 20 min · everyone runs it

The lab below starts with brief, voice and specifics on and structure deliberately off. That combination is the most instructive state in the whole lab, because the content is genuinely good - real numbers, real names, your register - and it is welded into a wall. Toggle structure on and off and watch two rows in particular: rhythm and reading grade. The words barely change. The shape does.

Read the starting draft aloud. Good facts, right register, and no way in. Note how similar every sentence length is, and how long it takes you to find the ask.

Turn Structure on. Watch the rhythm row move and the reading grade fall. On the ladder this rung is only worth two points on the total, 68 to 70, which is exactly the point being made.

Turn it off again and time yourself finding the ask. Then on again, and time it. That gap is the whole value of the lever, and no measure in the scorecard can see it.

Now add Cut on top of the structured version to reach 98. Notice how much easier cutting is once the shape exists: you delete whole blocks rather than negotiating with individual sentences.

Press Sound more professional last. Watch the shape survive and the content evaporate, 36 points gone. Structure alone does not save a memo from evasion.

What is written and what is measured The drafts are written examples - one good version, plus one specific kind of damage for each missing practice. Every number in the scorecard is computed from the text above it: hedge and AI-tell counts against real word lists, passive hits by pattern, sentence-length spread, nominalisation density, a real Flesch-Kincaid grade with syllable counting, and the share of sentences carrying a number or a name. The passive check is a heuristic - a be-verb followed by a participle - so read its count as a flag rather than a census.

Then the full climb, 48 → 52 → 68 → 70 → 83 → 98 with the words falling from 223 to 102, and the box you keep. Paste your last real status update into the second box, and read the specifics row before the total.

Real world

The weekly update that nobody reads is usually a governance problem wearing a writing costume. Someone somewhere asked for a weekly update, so a weekly update is produced whether or not anything happened. Within two months it has become a list of activity, because activity is always available and change is not. Within four, the readers have learned to skip it, which means the one week something genuinely urgent happens, the message lands in a document they no longer open. The writing fix is the hard rule: no material change means one line, or nothing at all. The real fix is going back to whoever asked for the weekly cadence and offering them an update on change instead of an update on schedule. That conversation is uncomfortable and takes ten minutes, and it is worth more than any amount of better prose.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Write the five scratch lines - point, three things, ask - for something you owe someone this week, before drafting a word of it. Time how long the drafting then takes compared with your usual.
  • Take your last three status updates and mark every sentence as change, meaning or ask. Anything that is none of the three is activity. Delete it and see what is left.
  • Write the decision memo for the Cadence education question using all five blocks, including the honest unknown with a named test. Keep it under 400 words. If you have taken the AI + Research course, use the same case and compare the two memos.
  • Find one unknown in your own recent writing that you softened. Rewrite it as mark, price, test - three sentences - and notice whether the honest version is shorter, which it almost always is.
